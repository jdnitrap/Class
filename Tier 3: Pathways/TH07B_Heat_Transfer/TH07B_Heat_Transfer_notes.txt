HEAT TRANSFER FUNDAMENTALS AND THREE MODES:

HEAT TRANSFER DEFINITION:
Heat transfer is the transport of thermal energy (heat) from a region of higher temperature to a region of lower temperature. This occurs spontaneously due to the second law of thermodynamics - entropy increases as energy flows from hot to cold.

FUNDAMENTAL PRINCIPLE:
Heat always flows from higher temperature to lower temperature
No net heat flow between objects at same temperature (thermal equilibrium)
Heat flow rate depends on temperature difference magnitude
Larger ΔT = faster heat flow

THREE BASIC MODES OF HEAT TRANSFER:

MODE 1: CONDUCTION (Molecular Interaction)
Definition: Heat transfer through a solid or stationary fluid by direct molecular contact.

Physical Mechanism:
• Thermal energy transfers through random molecular motion
• In solids: Atoms vibrate in fixed positions, vibrational energy propagates through structure
• In gases/liquids: Molecules collide, transferring kinetic energy through collisions
• NO bulk fluid motion required
• Requires physical contact between hot and cold regions

Fourier's Law of Conduction (Heat Conduction Equation):
Q = k × A × ΔT / L

Where:
• Q = heat transfer rate [Btu/h, kW]
• k = thermal conductivity of material [Btu/(hr·ft·°F), W/(m·K)]
• A = cross-sectional area perpendicular to heat flow [ft², m²]
• ΔT = temperature difference [°F, °C, K]
• L = thickness of material (distance heat travels) [ft, m]

Physical Interpretation:
• Larger k: Material conducts heat better (higher thermal conductivity)
• Larger A: More area for heat flow (increased rate)
• Larger ΔT: Stronger driving force (increased rate)
• Larger L: More distance for heat to travel (decreased rate)

Example Calculation - Heat Through Boiler Tube Wall:
Given:
• Tube material: Carbon steel (k = 30 Btu/(hr·ft·°F))
• Tube diameter: 1 inch outer, 0.84 inch inner (wall thickness L = 0.08 inch = 0.0067 ft)
• Tube length: 40 feet
• Area: πDL = π × (0.92 inch) × 40 ft = 120 ft² (mean diameter)
• Hot side: T₁ = 600°F (combustion gas side)
• Cold side: T₂ = 500°F (water side inside tube)
• ΔT = 100°F

Heat flow:
Q = 30 × 120 × 100 / 0.0067 = 53.7 million Btu/h per tube!

This shows even thin tube walls conduct enormous amounts of heat when area is large.

MODE 2: CONVECTION (Fluid Motion + Conduction)
Definition: Heat transfer between a solid surface and an adjacent fluid (liquid or gas) due to fluid motion combined with conduction.

Physical Mechanism:
• Fluid near hot surface absorbs heat by conduction
• Heated fluid expands (becomes less dense)
• Buoyancy forces cause hot fluid to move away
• Cooler fluid replaces hot fluid at surface
• Bulk fluid motion (circulation) combined with molecular conduction
• Much more efficient than pure conduction because bulk motion carries energy

Boundary Layer Concept:
Thermal boundary layer is a thin layer of fluid immediately adjacent to the surface
• Within this layer: Fluid velocity changes from surface velocity (zero for stationary surface) to bulk flow velocity
• Heat conduction dominant within boundary layer
• Outside boundary layer: Heat transfer by bulk convection
• Thinner boundary layer → Higher heat transfer coefficient → Better cooling

Factors Affecting Convection Heat Transfer:
1. Temperature difference (ΔT): Larger difference → faster heat transfer (linear relationship)
2. Flow rate of fluid: Higher velocity → thinner boundary layer → better heat transfer
3. Surface roughness: Rough surface thickens turbulence → better heat transfer
4. Fluid properties: Density, viscosity, specific heat, thermal conductivity
5. Surface area: Larger area → more heat transfer (linear relationship)
6. Phase change: Boiling or condensation dramatically improves heat transfer (latent heat)

TWO TYPES OF CONVECTION:

Natural Convection:
• Caused by density differences due to temperature gradients
• Hot fluid rises, cold fluid sinks (buoyancy)
• No pump or external force needed
• Occurs even without any applied flow
• Slower than forced convection
• Example: Heat rising from hot pipe to ceiling, natural circulation in reactor

Forced Convection:
• Caused by external pump/fan forcing fluid over surface
• Higher velocity → much thinner boundary layer → better heat transfer
• Much more efficient than natural convection
• Easier to control and predict
• Most common in power plants
• Example: Cooling water pumped through condenser tubes, reactor coolant pumped through core

Newton's Law of Cooling (Convection Heat Transfer):
Q = h × A × ΔT

Where:
• Q = heat transfer rate [Btu/h, kW]
• h = convection heat transfer coefficient [Btu/(hr·ft²·°F), W/(m²·K)]
• A = surface area [ft², m²]
• ΔT = temperature difference between surface and bulk fluid [°F, K]

Physical Interpretation:
• h encompasses all fluid motion and property effects
• Larger h: Better boundary layer (thinner, turbulent) → higher heat transfer
• Larger A: More surface area → more total heat transfer
• Larger ΔT: Stronger driving force → more heat transfer

Typical h Values (Btu/(hr·ft²·°F)):
• Natural convection (air): 1-5
• Forced convection (air, moderate velocity): 5-100
• Forced convection (water, moderate velocity): 100-1000
• Forced convection (water, high velocity): 1000-5000
• Boiling water: 1000-10,000+ (very high due to latent heat)
• Condensing steam: 1000-5000

Mode 3: RADIATION (Electromagnetic Waves)
Definition: Heat transfer via electromagnetic waves (infrared radiation) between surfaces without requiring a medium.

Physical Mechanism:
• All matter above absolute zero radiates thermal energy
• Radiation energy carries heat from surface to surface through space
• Does NOT require matter (works in vacuum)
• No conduction or convection required
• Wavelength depends on temperature (hotter objects emit shorter wavelengths, higher visible light content)

Stefan-Boltzmann Law (Radiation Heat Transfer):
Q = σ × A × ε × (T⁴)

More precisely:
Q = σ × A × ε × (T_hot⁴ - T_cold⁴)

Where:
• Q = heat transfer rate [Btu/h, kW]
• σ = Stefan-Boltzmann constant = 0.1714 × 10⁻⁸ Btu/(hr·ft²·°R⁴) = 5.67 × 10⁻⁸ W/(m²·K⁴)
• A = surface area [ft², m²]
• ε = emissivity (fraction of maximum radiation emitted, 0-1) [dimensionless]
• T = absolute temperature [°R or K]

Critical Feature: Temperature to FOURTH POWER (T⁴)
This makes radiation extremely sensitive to temperature

Radiation at Various Temperatures:

Room temperature (70°F = 530°R):
Q_room = 0.1714 × 10⁻⁸ × 1 × 1 × (530)⁴ ≈ 184 Btu/(hr·ft²)

Moderate temperature (212°F = 672°R):
Q_mod = 0.1714 × 10⁻⁸ × 1 × 1 × (672)⁴ ≈ 366 Btu/(hr·ft²)

High temperature (1000°F = 1460°R):
Q_high = 0.1714 × 10⁻⁸ × 1 × 1 × (1460)⁴ ≈ 5,266 Btu/(hr·ft²)

Key Observation: Doubling absolute temperature increases radiation by 16×!

Emissivity (ε) Values (at high temperature):
• Polished metals: 0.05-0.20 (shiny, poor emitters)
• Oxidized/painted metals: 0.30-0.90 (poor condition, good emitters)
• Concrete/brick: 0.85-0.95
• Black paint/soot: 0.95-0.98 (excellent emitters)
• Bare steel (rough): 0.80-0.90
• Stainless steel (oxidized): 0.60-0.80

Note: Good absorbers are also good emitters (Kirchhoff's law)

IMPORTANCE IN NUCLEAR POWER PLANTS:

Normal Operation:
• Conduction: Dominant through fuel, cladding, tube walls (10-20% of total)
• Convection: Dominant between fuel/surfaces and coolant (80-90% of total)
• Radiation: Negligible during normal operation (surfaces at moderate temperatures)

Accident Conditions (Loss of Coolant Accident - LOCA):
• Conduction: Still through fuel and cladding (limited path as coolant lost)
• Convection: Severely reduced or eliminated as coolant lost
• Radiation: BECOMES CRITICAL for cooling exposed core
• At 1800-2200°F fuel temperature, radiation becomes major cooling mechanism
• Zirconium cladding can oxidize rapidly at high temperature, releasing heat
• Radiation cooling of exposed core is last line of defense before meltdown

The Challenge:
• Radiation cooling only effective when fuel very hot (inefficient at normal temps)
• But becomes primary cooling method if active convection lost
• System designed to maintain liquid cooling (convection dominant)
• Radiation is backup, not primary

SPECIFIC HEAT CAPACITY:

DEFINITION:
Specific heat capacity (cp) is the amount of heat required to raise the temperature of ONE UNIT MASS of a substance by ONE DEGREE TEMPERATURE.

Mathematical Definition:
cp = Q / (m × ΔT)

Rearranging:
Q = m × cp × ΔT

Where:
• Q = total heat transferred [Btu, J]
• m = mass of substance [lbm, kg]
• cp = specific heat capacity [Btu/(lbm·°F), J/(kg·K)]
• ΔT = temperature change [°F, K]

Units (English System):
Btu/(lbm·°F) - Heat in Btu needed per pound-mass per degree Fahrenheit
This is equivalent to saying: 1 Btu raises 1 lbm of water by 1°F

STANDARD REFERENCE:
One Btu is defined such that 1 Btu raises 1 lbm of WATER by 1°F at standard conditions
Therefore: cp_water = 1.0 Btu/(lbm·°F) by definition

Other Substances Compared to Water:
• Air: cp ≈ 0.24 Btu/(lbm·°F) (about 1/4 of water)
• Steel: cp ≈ 0.12 Btu/(lbm·°F) (about 1/8 of water)
• Aluminum: cp ≈ 0.22 Btu/(lbm·°F) (about 1/4 of water)
• Steam (at constant pressure): cp ≈ 0.50 Btu/(lbm·°F)
• Concrete: cp ≈ 0.20 Btu/(lbm·°F)

Physical Meaning:
Water has relatively high specific heat capacity
• Requires more energy to change water temperature than most other substances
• Water stores more thermal energy per unit mass than alternatives
• Makes water excellent coolant (absorbs lots of heat without large temperature rise)

FOR FLOWING SYSTEMS (Power Balance Equation):
Q̇ = ṁ × cp × ΔT

Where:
• Q̇ = heat transfer rate [Btu/h, kW]
• ṁ = mass flow rate [lbm/h, lbm/s, kg/s]
• cp = specific heat [Btu/(lbm·°F), J/(kg·K)]
• ΔT = temperature change [°F, K]

Example - Reactor Coolant System Heat Balance:
Given:
• Primary coolant flow: ṁ = 150,000 lbm/h
• Specific heat: cp = 1.0 Btu/(lbm·°F)
• Coolant temperature: Inlet (Tc) = 550°F, Outlet (Th) = 600°F
• Temperature rise: ΔT = 600 - 550 = 50°F

Heat transferred from core:
Q̇ = 150,000 × 1.0 × 50 = 7,500,000 Btu/h = 2,195 kW ≈ 2.2 MW

This 2.2 MW represents the thermal power (also called Core Thermal Power or CTP)

TEMPERATURE DEPENDENCE:
Specific heat capacity varies slightly with temperature and pressure
For water/steam at different temperatures:
• Liquid water 32-212°F: cp ≈ 1.0 Btu/(lbm·°F) (relatively constant)
• Liquid water 200-400°F: cp ≈ 1.0 Btu/(lbm·°F) (very constant)
• Superheated steam: cp varies with T and P (100-2000°F: cp = 0.4-0.6)

For engineering purposes:
Use constant cp approximation for moderate temperature ranges
More accurate: Use steam tables to find h values directly

IMPORTANCE FOR HEAT BALANCE CALCULATIONS:
1. Allows calculation of heat removed by coolant
2. Thermal power = ṁ × cp × ΔT (primary heat balance method)
3. Comparison of primary and secondary side heat balances validates system performance
4. Changes in cp (due to composition changes) detected through heat balance monitoring

CONDUCTION HEAT TRANSFER THROUGH MATERIALS:

CONDUCTION DEFINITION REVIEW:
Heat transfer through solid material by molecular contact without bulk fluid motion.
Mechanism: Thermal vibrations propagate through atomic/molecular bonds.

FOURIER'S LAW (HEAT CONDUCTION EQUATION) DETAILED:
Q = k × A × ΔT / L

Derivation from First Principles:
Heat flow rate proportional to:
• Thermal conductivity k: Material's ability to conduct heat
• Area A: Cross-sectional area perpendicular to heat flow
• Temperature gradient: ΔT/L (temperature change over distance)

Rearranging:
Q/A = (k × ΔT) / L = heat flux [Btu/(hr·ft²)]
(Heat flux = heat transfer rate per unit area)

THERMAL CONDUCTIVITY (k) - KEY MATERIAL PROPERTY:

Definition: Amount of heat that flows through a unit area of material, per unit thickness, per unit temperature difference in one unit time.

Units: Btu/(hr·ft·°F) or W/(m·K)

Physical Meaning:
• Higher k = Better conductor (heat flows easily)
• Lower k = Worse conductor (heat blocked)

Factors Affecting k:
1. Material type (atomic/molecular structure)
2. Temperature (k usually decreases with increasing T for metals)
3. Crystalline structure (affects energy propagation)
4. Porosity (voids reduce effective conductivity)
5. Moisture content (especially in insulators)

THERMAL CONDUCTIVITY VALUES FOR MATERIALS:

Metals (Excellent Conductors):
• Copper: 226 Btu/(hr·ft·°F) - Best conductor, used for high heat transfer
• Aluminum: 128 Btu/(hr·ft·°F) - Excellent, used in radiators
• Iron/Steel: 30-40 Btu/(hr·ft·°F) - Good, used in boiler tubes
• Stainless Steel: 8-14 Btu/(hr·ft·°F) - Moderate, used in nuclear systems

Alloys (Moderate Conductors):
• 90-10 Copper-Nickel: 23 Btu/(hr·ft·°F)
• 416 Stainless Steel: 14 Btu/(hr·ft·°F)
• 316 Stainless Steel: 9.4 Btu/(hr·ft·°F)
• Inconel 600: 8.6 Btu/(hr·ft·°F)
• Inconel 690: 7.8 Btu/(hr·ft·°F)
• Zirconium alloys: ~12 Btu/(hr·ft·°F)

Insulators (Poor Conductors):
• Rock wool: 0.03 Btu/(hr·ft·°F)
• Concrete: 0.2-0.7 Btu/(hr·ft·°F)
• Air (at atmospheric pressure): 0.0145 Btu/(hr·ft·°F)
• Saturated steam @ 600°F: 0.0301 Btu/(hr·ft·°F)

Dramatic Difference:
Copper (226) is ~7500× better conductor than air (0.03)!

Temperature Dependence Examples:

Copper thermal conductivity varies with temperature:
• 32°F: 224 Btu/(hr·ft·°F)
• 212°F: 219 Btu/(hr·ft·°F)
• 500°F: 213 Btu/(hr·ft·°F)
Generally decreases ~3-5% per 100°F for metals

Stainless Steel 304 (typical nuclear plant material):
• 68°F: 8.3 Btu/(hr·ft·°F)
• 200°F: 8.8 Btu/(hr·ft·°F)
• 400°F: 9.6 Btu/(hr·ft·°F)
• 600°F: 10.2 Btu/(hr·ft·°F)
Increases slightly with temperature (opposite of copper)

CONDUCTION RESISTANCE:
Thermal resistance analogy (similar to electrical resistance):

Thermal resistance:
R_thermal = L / (k × A)

Heat flow:
Q = ΔT / R_thermal

Advantages of resistance concept:
• Multiple layers in series: R_total = R₁ + R₂ + R₃ + ...
• Multiple paths in parallel: 1/R_total = 1/R₁ + 1/R₂ + ...
• Easy to add/remove layers and recalculate

EXAMPLE - COMPOSITE WALL (Boiler Tube):
Three layers:
1. Tube wall (steel): k₁ = 30, L₁ = 0.05 inch = 0.00417 ft, A = 10 ft²
2. Scale/deposits: k₂ = 0.5 (very poor), L₂ = 0.01 inch = 0.00083 ft, A = 10 ft²
3. Corrosion products: k₃ = 1.0, L₃ = 0.002 inch = 0.000167 ft, A = 10 ft²

Resistances:
R₁ = 0.00417 / (30 × 10) = 1.39 × 10⁻⁵ hr·°F/Btu
R₂ = 0.00083 / (0.5 × 10) = 1.66 × 10⁻⁴ hr·°F/Btu (12× larger than steel!)
R₃ = 0.000167 / (1.0 × 10) = 1.67 × 10⁻⁵ hr·°F/Btu

Total resistance:
R_total = 1.39 × 10⁻⁵ + 1.66 × 10⁻⁴ + 1.67 × 10⁻⁵ = 2.03 × 10⁻⁴ hr·°F/Btu

Temperature difference of 100°F:
Q = 100 / (2.03 × 10⁻⁴) = 492,600 Btu/h

Without deposits (only steel and corrosion):
R_total = 1.39 × 10⁻⁵ + 1.67 × 10⁻⁵ = 3.06 × 10⁻⁵
Q = 100 / (3.06 × 10⁻⁵) = 3,267,000 Btu/h

RESULT: Deposits reduced heat transfer by factor of 6.6× (or 85% reduction)
This demonstrates the huge impact of fouling in heat exchangers!

Choosing Material Requires Trade-offs:

For Maximum Heat Transfer (k):
• Copper: Excellent conductor, but soften at high T, poor corrosion resistance
• Aluminum: Excellent conductor, but poor at elevated T, limited steam side use

For Corrosion Resistance:
• Stainless Steel: Good corrosion resistance at elevated T, but lower conductivity
• Zirconium alloys: Excellent at high T, but complex material
• Copper-Nickel: Good corrosion resistance, moderate conductivity

For Nuclear Plant Applications (316 Stainless Steel Preferred):
• Corrosion resistance in high-temperature water
• Resistance to stress corrosion cracking
• Strength at elevated temperature maintained
• Cost-effectiveness
• Trade-off: Moderately lower conductivity than carbon steel, but adequate with convection

Design Implication:
Cannot choose highest k material (copper) due to corrosion
Must compensate with enhanced convection (higher flow rates, better geometry)
Heat transfer equation: Q = U × A × ΔT encompasses all effects

CONVECTION HEAT TRANSFER DETAILED:

CONVECTION DEFINITION AND MECHANISM REVIEW:
Heat transfer from a hot surface to an adjacent fluid (or vice versa) through the combination of conduction within a thin boundary layer and bulk fluid motion.

BOUNDARY LAYER CONCEPT:

Physical Structure:
Immediately adjacent to any solid surface in contact with flowing fluid, a thin layer develops:
• Thermal boundary layer: Region where temperature changes from surface temp to bulk fluid temp
• Hydrodynamic boundary layer: Region where velocity changes from zero (no slip at surface) to bulk velocity

Boundary Layer Thickness (δ):
• Typically 0.01 to 0.1 inches in practical systems
• Decreases with increased flow rate (faster flow → thinner layer)
• Increases with increased fluid viscosity
• Increases downstream as layer develops

Temperature Profile in Boundary Layer:
• At surface: T = T_surface (no slip condition)
• At edge of boundary layer: T = T_bulk (free stream condition)
• Across layer: Temperature decreases from surface to bulk
• Gradient very steep near surface, gradually flattens approaching bulk

Heat Transfer Within Boundary Layer:
• Right at surface: Heat transfer by pure conduction (v = 0, stationary fluid)
• Moving outward: Heat transfer by conduction + convection (both mechanisms)
• At layer edge: Heat transfer primarily by bulk convection

EFFECT OF FLOW RATE ON HEAT TRANSFER:

Low Flow Rate (Laminar or Slow Flow):
• Boundary layer thick
• Little turbulent mixing
• Temperature gradient shallow
• Convection coefficient h SMALL
• Example: Water at 1 ft/s in large pipe

High Flow Rate (Turbulent Flow):
• Boundary layer thin
• Vigorous turbulent mixing (eddies constantly scrape off layer)
• Temperature gradient steep (thin layer with ΔT across it)
• Convection coefficient h LARGE
• Example: Water at 20 ft/s in same pipe

Flow Acceleration Effects:
Doubling flow velocity roughly:
• Halves boundary layer thickness (thinner)
• Increases heat transfer coefficient h by ~20-50% depending on flow regime
• Increases turbulence and mixing intensity

Mathematical relationship:
h ∝ (velocity)^n where 0.4 < n < 0.8 depending on flow type
(Not linear, but very sensitive)

SURFACE ROUGHNESS EFFECTS:

Smooth Surface (polished pipe):
• Boundary layer reaches full development
• Smooth streamlines in sublayer
• Moderate h

Rough Surface (corroded, scale deposits):
• Roughness elements disturb boundary layer
• Promotes turbulence even at layer edge
• Thins effective layer thickness
• Increases h significantly (20-100% in extreme cases)

Engineering Implication:
Slight corrosion/deposits initially improve heat transfer (roughness effect)
But deposits accumulate further → reduce k dramatically (fouling effect)
Net result: Overall h decreases as time progresses

FACTORS AFFECTING CONVECTION COEFFICIENT (h):

1. Flow Rate Effect (dominant):
   h increases with velocity (h ∝ v^0.5 to v^0.8)

2. Fluid Properties:
   • Density (ρ): Higher density → higher h
   • Viscosity (μ): Higher viscosity → lower h (harder to circulate)
   • Specific heat (cp): Higher cp → higher h (fluid absorbs more heat per degree)
   • Thermal conductivity (k_fluid): Higher k → higher h

3. Geometry:
   • Pipe diameter: Smaller diameter → higher h (due to boundary layer thinning relative to pipe size)
   • Surface area: More area → more total heat transfer (but h itself unchanged)
   • Entrance vs. developed flow: h higher at entrance (developing boundary layer)

4. Temperature:
   • Fluid properties change with T
   • h at 100°F ≠ h at 500°F for same flow rate
   • Must account for property changes in design

5. Phase Changes:
   • Boiling dramatically increases h (see later)
   • Condensation also increases h

NATURAL CONVECTION (Buoyancy-Driven):
Mechanism:
• Hot fluid becomes less dense (expands)
• Buoyancy forces cause hot fluid to rise
• Gravity brings cold fluid down to replace it
• Circulation pattern develops without pump

Characteristics:
• Much slower than forced convection
• h typically 1-10 Btu/(hr·ft²·°F) for air, 100-500 for water
• Occurs even with system shutdown (as long as temperature differences exist)
• Provides backup cooling if forced circulation lost

Power Plant Examples:
• Natural convection in reactor vessel (before pump starts)
• Stack draft carrying hot gases upward (cooling tower fan off)
• Decay heat removal by natural circulation (severe accident scenario)

Importance in Severe Accidents:
If all pumps fail:
• Reactor coolant still circulates by natural convection
• Provides core cooling at reduced rate
• Removes decay heat and prevents meltdown (for several hours)
• Critical safety feature: passive cooling by buoyancy alone

FORCED CONVECTION (Pump/Fan-Driven):
Mechanism:
• Pump or fan forces fluid to move
• Bulk fluid motion carries hot fluid away from surface
• Much higher velocities than natural convection
• Boundary layer continuously scraped away, reformed

Characteristics:
• Much faster cooling than natural convection (100-1000× faster)
• h typically 100-10,000 Btu/(hr·ft²·°F) depending on conditions
• Requires mechanical power input (pump power)
• Precise control of flow rate

Power Plant Applications:
• Reactor coolant pump circulates primary coolant
• Feedwater pump pressurizes boiler feed
• Cooling tower fans force air through condenser cooling tubes
• Blower forces combustion air through furnace

NEWTON'S LAW OF COOLING (CONVECTION EQUATION):
Q̇ = h × A × ΔT

Where:
• Q̇ = heat transfer rate [Btu/h]
• h = convection heat transfer coefficient [Btu/(hr·ft²·°F)]
• A = surface area [ft²]
• ΔT = temperature difference between surface and bulk fluid [°F]

Physical Interpretation:
• Better heat transfer (higher h) comes from better boundary layer control
• Larger area (higher A) directly increases heat transfer (proportional)
• Larger temperature difference (higher ΔT) drives more heat transfer (proportional)

TYPICAL CONVECTION COEFFICIENTS (h) VALUES:

Natural Convection (No Pump):
• Air (natural): 1-5 Btu/(hr·ft²·°F)
• Water (natural): 20-100 Btu/(hr·ft²·°F)
• Oil (natural): 5-25 Btu/(hr·ft²·°F)

Forced Convection (With Pump/Fan):
• Air (5 ft/s): 5-10 Btu/(hr·ft²·°F)
• Air (20 ft/s): 20-50 Btu/(hr·ft²·°F)
• Water (5 ft/s): 500-2000 Btu/(hr·ft²·°F)
• Water (20 ft/s): 2000-5000 Btu/(hr·ft²·°F)

Phase Change Processes:
• Boiling water: 1000-10,000+ Btu/(hr·ft²·°F)
• Condensing steam: 1000-5000 Btu/(hr·ft²·°F)

Note: Water dramatically better coolant than air (10-100× higher h)

REYNOLDS NUMBER (Flow Type Indicator):
Re = ρ × v × D / μ

Where:
• ρ = density
• v = velocity
• D = pipe diameter
• μ = dynamic viscosity

Interpretation:
• Re < 2300: Laminar flow (smooth, orderly, lower h)
• 2300 < Re < 10,000: Transition
• Re > 10,000: Turbulent flow (chaotic, high mixing, higher h)

Most plant systems operate turbulent (high Re) for efficient cooling.

BOUNDARY LAYER THICKNESS AND EFFECTS:

BOUNDARY LAYER PHYSICS DETAILED:

Boundary Layer Definition:
Region near a solid surface where fluid velocity (and temperature in thermal boundary layer) transitions from surface conditions to free stream conditions.

Hydrodynamic Boundary Layer:
• Velocity boundary layer thickness (δ_v)
• Distance from surface where v reaches 99% of bulk velocity
• Caused by no-slip condition at wall (fluid touching surface stops)

Thermal Boundary Layer:
• Temperature boundary layer thickness (δ_T)
• Distance from surface where T reaches 99% of bulk temperature
• Related to but not identical with velocity boundary layer

Relationship Between Layers:
δ_T ≈ δ_v × (Pr)^(-1/3)
Where Pr = Prandtl number = μ × cp / k
For water: Pr ≈ 5-10 (thermal layer smaller than velocity layer)
For air: Pr ≈ 0.7 (thermal layer larger than velocity layer)

BOUNDARY LAYER DEVELOPMENT:

At Entrance to Pipe:
• Fresh pipe section
• Developing boundary layer (growing from zero thickness)
• Steep temperature gradient near wall → High h
• h much higher than fully developed section

Developing Length:
Hydrodynamic: L_h ≈ 0.05 × Re × D ≈ 0.05 × Re × D
Thermal: L_th ≈ 0.05 × Re × Pr × D ≈ 0.05 × Re × Pr × D

Example - Water at 10 ft/s in 1-inch pipe:
Re ≈ 100,000 (turbulent)
L_h ≈ 0.05 × 100,000 × (1/12) ≈ 420 inches ≈ 35 feet
Development takes significant pipe length!

In Fully Developed Region:
• Boundary layer equilibrium reached
• No new development occurring
• h constant along pipe
• More predictable and easier to analyze
• Most of pipe length operates in fully developed regime

Wall Effect on Heat Transfer (Heat Flux):

Heat flux at wall: q"_w = h × (T_surface - T_bulk)

Local vs. Average:
• Local h varies along pipe (higher at entrance, lower downstream)
• Average h used for total heat transfer calculations
• Design typically based on average conditions

Mixing Effects:
Turbulent mixing continuously disrupts boundary layer
• Eddies bring cold fluid from bulk toward surface
• Hot fluid from surface mixed outward
• Net effect: Thinner effective thermal layer
• Higher effective h than laminar flow

Laminar vs. Turbulent Convection Coefficients:
Same pipe, same flow rate difference:
• Laminar: h ≈ 100 Btu/(hr·ft²·°F)
• Turbulent: h ≈ 1000+ Btu/(hr·ft²·°F)
• Turbulent 10× better! Main reason systems designed for turbulent flow

EFFECT OF SURFACE ROUGHNESS ON BOUNDARY LAYER:

Hydraulically Smooth Surface (polished):
• Roughness elements smaller than sublayer thickness
• Surface appears smooth to flow
• Boundary layer develops normally
• Standard smooth-pipe equations apply

Hydraulically Rough Surface (corroded, scaled):
• Roughness elements protrude beyond sublayer
• Disrupts smooth velocity profile
• Increases turbulence production
• Thins effective boundary layer
• Increases h by 20-100%

Initial Deposits Effect:
Freshly deposited scale might increase h:
• Roughness elements promote turbulence
• Layer still relatively thin
• Net h might be higher than clean surface!

Over Time (Fouling):
As deposits accumulate:
• Thermal conductivity of deposits decreases
• k_deposits << k_metal (very important effect)
• Deposit layer grows thicker
• Poor conduction through thick deposit layer
• Overall U decreases dramatically (k effect dominates over h increase)

Example from Note 8:
2 mil (0.002 inch) of scale:
• Initial increase in h from roughness: ~10-15%
• But adds thick layer with poor k
• Net effect: Overall U drops ~30% due to conduction resistance through scale

CONVECTION COEFFICIENT ESTIMATION:

For turbulent flow in circular pipe (Dittus-Boelert equation):
Nu = 0.023 × Re^0.8 × Pr^0.4

Where:
Nu = Nusselt number = h × D / k_fluid

Rearranging:
h = (0.023 × Re^0.8 × Pr^0.4 × k_fluid) / D

For water at typical conditions:
h increases as flow velocity increases (v^0.8 dependence)

Doubling velocity:
h increases by 2^0.8 ≈ 1.74× (nearly double)

Physical Interpretation:
Increasing flow thins boundary layer
Thinner layer increases temperature gradient near wall
Higher gradient → faster heat transfer
Linear relationships assumed, but actual dependence is power law

HEAT EXCHANGER HEAT TRANSFER MECHANISMS:

HEAT EXCHANGER ARCHITECTURE:

Three Heat Transfer Resistances in Series:

Path: Hot Fluid → Tube → Cold Fluid

Step 1: Hot Fluid to Tube Inner Wall
• Convective heat transfer from hot fluid to tube (h_hot coefficient)
• Resistance: R_hot = 1 / (h_hot × A_inner)
• Example: Hot combustion gas to boiler tube

Step 2: Conduction Through Tube Wall
• Conductive heat transfer through metal tube
• Resistance: R_wall = L / (k × A_mean)
• Example: Through carbon steel or stainless steel tube wall

Step 3: Tube Inner Wall to Cold Fluid
• Convective heat transfer from tube to cold fluid (h_cold coefficient)
• Resistance: R_cold = 1 / (h_cold × A_outer)
• Example: From inner tube surface to cooling water

TOTAL THERMAL RESISTANCE:
R_total = R_hot + R_wall + R_cold

Heat transfer rate:
Q̇ = ΔT_total / R_total = ΔT_total / (R_hot + R_wall + R_cold)

OVERALL HEAT TRANSFER COEFFICIENT (U_o):
Definition: Single combined coefficient representing all three resistance effects

Q̇ = U_o × A_o × ΔT_log_mean

Where:
• U_o = overall heat transfer coefficient [Btu/(hr·ft²·°F)]
• A_o = outer surface area [ft²]
• ΔT_log_mean = logarithmic mean temperature difference [°F]

Relationship to individual resistances:
1 / U_o = R_total × A_o = (1/(h_hot × A_i) + L/(k×A_m) + 1/(h_cold × A_o)) × A_o

Simplified (when areas similar):
1/U_o ≈ 1/h_hot + (L×A_o)/(k×A_m) + 1/h_cold

WHY SERIES RESISTANCES MATTER:

Each Resistance Contributes to Total:
If one resistance very large, it dominates and limits total heat transfer

Example - Boiler Tube:
h_hot = 50 (combustion gas side, poor h)
k = 30, L = 0.05", A ratio ≈ 1 (similar)
h_cold = 2000 (water boiling side, excellent h)

Resistances (per unit area):
R_hot = 1/50 = 0.020
R_wall = 0.05 / (30 × 1) = 0.0017 (negligible!)
R_cold = 1/2000 = 0.0005 (negligible!)
Total = 0.020 + 0.0017 + 0.0005 = 0.0222

Dominant resistance: 90% is R_hot

Design Implication:
Improve h_hot by increasing combustion gas velocity (most important)
Decreasing wall thickness helps slightly
Improving h_cold (already excellent) helps minimally

DOMINANT RESISTANCES IN DIFFERENT EQUIPMENT:

Boiler:
• Hot side (combustion gas): Poor h → dominant resistance
• Tube wall: Small resistance (thin, good k)
• Cold side (water/steam): Good to excellent h
• Design strategy: Maximize convection on hot side (gas velocity, tube arrangement)

Condenser:
• Hot side (steam): Excellent h from condensation
• Tube wall: Small resistance
• Cold side (cooling water): Good h, moderately turbulent flow
• Design strategy: Maximize cooling water flow for h_cold (usually lower than condensing h)

Feedwater Heater (single-phase):
• Hot side (steam): Excellent h from condensation
• Tube wall: Small resistance
• Cold side (water): Good h, but usually lower than hot side
• Design strategy: Balance both sides, often cold side is limiting

MONITORING U_o FOR EQUIPMENT CONDITION:

Why Monitor U_o:
U_o changes with equipment condition:
• Clean tube: U_o high
• Fouled tube: U_o drops
• Deposits add thick resistance layer

Calculation:
Q̇ = U_o × A × ΔT_mean

If measure Q̇ (by heat balance), A (fixed), ΔT (measure temperatures):
U_o = Q̇ / (A × ΔT)

If U_o drops from initial design value:
• Fouling layer present (add deposits on tube wall)
• Air/non-condensables blocking flow (reduced h due to blockage)
• Tube erosion (changing geometry)
• Corrosion layer forming (poor k resistance)

Design Value vs. Actual:
U_o_design = 1000 Btu/(hr·ft²·°F) (clean, new tubes)
U_o_actual_measured = 700 Btu/(hr·ft²·°F) (after 5 years operation)
Degradation = (1000-700)/1000 = 30%

FOULING RESISTANCE:
Deposits on tubes add resistance layer:
R_foul = L_foul / k_foul

Deposit Characteristics:
• Low thermal conductivity (poor conductor): k ≈ 0.1-1 Btu/(hr·ft·°F)
• Thickness grows over time: L increases with operation

Example from Note 8:
2 mil (0.002 inch) scale deposit:
• k_scale ≈ 0.5 Btu/(hr·ft·°F)
• R_scale = 0.002 / (0.5 × A) = 0.004/A
• Huge resistance compared to tube wall!

Impact on Overall U_o:
Before fouling: U_o = 1000 Btu/(hr·ft²·°F)
After 2 mil scale: U_o = 700 Btu/(hr·ft²·°F) (30% reduction)

This is why condenser cleaning critical to maintain efficiency!

[Note ID: 8]
OVERALL HEAT TRANSFER COEFFICIENT AND MONITORING:

DEFINITION REVIEW:
U_o is single combined coefficient including effects of:
• Hot-side convection (h_hot)
• Tube wall conduction (k and thickness)
• Cold-side convection (h_cold)
• Any fouling deposits
• Any non-condensables blocking flow

EQUATION:
Q̇ = U_o × A_o × ΔT_mean

Practical use:
U_o = Q̇ / (A_o × ΔT_mean)

When Q̇, A, and ΔT known from measurements/design, calculate actual U_o

MONITORING U_o AS CONDITION INDICATOR:

Strategy:
Track U_o over time for heat exchanger
Compare to design baseline
Drops indicate fouling or other degradation

Baseline Values (typical):
Boiler: U_o ≈ 20-50 Btu/(hr·ft²·°F) (limited by gas-side h)
Condenser: U_o ≈ 500-1000 Btu/(hr·ft²·°F) (high due to condensation)
Feedwater heater: U_o ≈ 100-500 Btu/(hr·ft²·°F)

Rate of Change Important:
• Slow degradation (1-5% per year): Normal, plan maintenance
• Rapid degradation (>10% per month): Emergency condition, plan cleaning

Causes of U_o Degradation:

1. Fouling (Most Common):
Deposits on tube inner or outer surface
• Mineral scale (calcium, magnesium salts)
• Corrosion products (iron oxides)
• Biological growth (algae, bacteria in cooling water)
• Oil/silts from leaking seals

2. Air/Non-Condensable Gases:
• Air in condenser (should be evacuated during startup)
• Released gases (oxygen from feedwater, CO₂ from chemistry)
• Hydrogen from radiolysis or corrosion
• These gases form blanket on tubes, reducing steam contact
• Locally block steam access to tube → Poor condensation

3. Tube Erosion:
• High-velocity steam can erode tubes
• Localized flow patterns cause erosion
• Changes tube geometry, reduces effective U_o

4. Corrosion Layer:
• Corrosion product buildup
• Acts like poor-conducting deposit
• Thickness increases over time

5. Mud Balls (Condenser):
• Particles from cooling water settle in tubes
• Form deposits that partially block flow
• Reduce tube effective area and cooling

FOULING DEPOSIT THICKNESS EFFECT:

Critical Observation:
Small amount of scale creates LARGE resistance increase

Example Calculation:
Condenser tube copper-nickel alloy
k = 23 Btu/(hr·ft·°F)
L_tube wall = 0.035 inch = 0.00292 ft

Resistance of tube wall:
R_wall = 0.00292 / 23 = 0.000127 hr·°F/Btu (negligible)

Now add scale:
k_scale = 0.5 Btu/(hr·ft·°F) (poor conductor)
L_scale = 0.002 inch = 0.000167 ft

Resistance of scale:
R_scale = 0.000167 / 0.5 = 0.000334 hr·°F/Btu

Ratio:
R_scale / R_wall = 0.000334 / 0.000127 = 2.6

Scale resistance is 2.6× LARGER than tube wall!

Effect on U_o:
If dominant resistance was cold-side h before:
1/U_o_original ≈ 0.001
Adding scale: 1/U_o_new ≈ 0.001 + 0.000334 = 0.001334
U_o_new / U_o_original = 1/1.334 = 0.75 = 75%

Result: 25% reduction in U_o from only 2 mils of scale

Even 1 mil deposit creates 12-15% degradation

This demonstrates:
Condenser requires frequent cleaning (2-5 years depending on water quality)
Heat transfer severely impacted by small fouling thickness
Plant efficiency directly dependent on condenser cleanliness

DESIGN COMPROMISE - U_o VALUE:

Why Not Over-Design with Huge U_o?
• Cost: Larger area or enhanced surfaces increase cost
• Diminishing returns: Last 10% of improvement costs 30-50% more
• Design life: Typical plants designed for 10-40 years of operation

Design U_o Selection:
• Conservative enough: Tolerate some fouling before cleaning necessary
• Aggressive enough: Minimize capital cost

Typical Design Practice:
• Assume baseline U_o (clean tubes)
• Apply fouling factor (estimate of expected degradation)
• Design U_o = U_o_baseline × fouling factor
• Example: If expect 20% fouling, design for 80% of clean U_o
• This ensures adequate heat transfer throughout design life between cleanings

MONITORING AND CONTROL:

How Operators Monitor U_o:
1. Calculate Q̇ from primary/secondary heat balance
2. Measure temperatures (T_in, T_out for both streams)
3. Know surface area A (fixed)
4. Calculate ΔT_mean (logarithmic mean)
5. Calculate U_o = Q̇ / (A × ΔT_mean)
6. Compare to design baseline
7. If U_o drops significantly: Plan cleaning or other corrective action

When to Take Action:
• <90% of design: Consider cleaning schedule
• <80% of design: Recommend immediate cleaning
• <70% of design: Emergency condition, impacts plant operation

For Condenser Specifically:
If U_o drops:
• First check: Is air/non-condensables present? (vent if needed)
• If air venting doesn't help: Schedule tube cleaning
• Cleaning involves: Stopping flow, sending brushes through tubes, flushing
• Expect 15-30% improvement in U_o after cleaning

RADIATION HEAT TRANSFER:

RADIATION DEFINITION REVIEW:
Heat transfer via electromagnetic waves (primarily infrared radiation) from one surface to another without requiring contact or an intermediate medium.

STEFAN-BOLTZMANN LAW:
Q̇ = σ × A × ε × (T_hot⁴ - T_cold⁴)

Where:
• Q̇ = heat transfer rate [Btu/h, kW]
• σ = Stefan-Boltzmann constant = 0.1714 × 10⁻⁸ Btu/(hr·ft²·°R⁴)
• A = radiating surface area [ft²]
• ε = emissivity (fraction between 0 and 1) [dimensionless]
• T_hot = absolute temperature of hot surface [°R]
• T_cold = absolute temperature of cold surface [°R]

Note: Temperature raised to FOURTH power (T⁴)
This makes radiation extremely temperature-dependent!

STEFAN-BOLTZMANN CONSTANT:
SI units: σ = 5.67 × 10⁻⁸ W/(m²·K⁴)
English units: σ = 0.1714 × 10⁻⁸ Btu/(hr·ft²·°R⁴)
Relationships: σ = 4.88 × 10⁻⁸ Btu/(hr·ft²·°R⁴) (sometimes used)

ABSOLUTE TEMPERATURE REQUIREMENT:
Radiation equation MUST use absolute temperature:
• Rankine (°R) in English system
• Kelvin (K) in SI system

Conversion:
°R = °F + 459.67
K = °C + 273.15

Example:
Room temperature: 70°F = 529.67°R ≈ 530°R
Hot surface: 300°F = 759.67°R ≈ 760°R

Critical Point:
Using relative temperatures (°F or °C) in radiation equation produces nonsense!

TEMPERATURE FOURTH-POWER EFFECT:

Radiation very sensitive to temperature:

Room temperature (70°F = 530°R):
Q̇ = 0.1714 × 10⁻⁸ × A × 1 × (530)⁴ ≈ 184 A Btu/(hr·ft²)
This is energy continuously radiated from every surface at room temperature!

Doubling absolute temperature (to 1060°R ≈ 600°F):
Q̇ = 0.1714 × 10⁻⁸ × A × 1 × (1060)⁴ ≈ 2,944 A Btu/(hr·ft²)
16× increase! (Because 2⁴ = 16)

Temperature sensitivities:
• Each 100°F increase → significant radiation increase (roughly 10-20% increase per 100°F)
• At high temperatures, small ΔT changes produce large radiation changes
• Critical during high-temperature accident scenarios

Example Power Plant Temperatures:

Normal operation:
• Primary coolant: 550°F (1009.67°R)
• Q_rad = 0.1714 × 10⁻⁸ × 1 × 1 × (1009.67)⁴ ≈ 1,070 Btu/(hr·ft²)
• Small radiation compared to convection (h ≈ 1000, so Q_conv ≈ 500,000+ Btu/(hr·ft²·°F) if ΔT = 100°F)

Loss of coolant accident:
• Fuel cladding: 2000°F (2459.67°R)
• Q_rad = 0.1714 × 10⁻⁸ × 1 × 1 × (2459.67)⁴ ≈ 44,400 Btu/(hr·ft²)
• Much larger! Radiation becomes significant cooling mechanism
• Comparable to what limited convection still available (no coolant to provide h)

EMISSIVITY (ε):

Definition: Fraction of maximum thermal radiation emitted by a surface.
• ε = 0: Perfect reflector (no radiation emitted)
• ε = 1: Perfect radiator (maximum radiation emitted)
• 0 < ε < 1: Real surfaces

Emissivity depends on:
1. Material type
2. Surface finish (oxidation, corrosion)
3. Temperature (changes with T)
4. Wavelength of radiation (spectral emissivity)

For practical calculations: Use total hemispherical emissivity

EMISSIVITY VALUES AT HIGH TEMPERATURE:

Polished Metals (Poor Radiators):
• Polished copper: ε ≈ 0.05-0.10
• Polished aluminum: ε ≈ 0.05-0.08
• Polished stainless steel: ε ≈ 0.15-0.20
• Polished iron: ε ≈ 0.10-0.20

Oxidized/Rough Metals (Better Radiators):
• Oxidized steel (rusty): ε ≈ 0.80-0.90
• Oxidized stainless steel: ε ≈ 0.60-0.80
• Weathered iron: ε ≈ 0.70-0.90
• Painted metals: ε ≈ 0.85-0.95

Non-Metals (Good Radiators):
• Black paint/soot: ε ≈ 0.95-0.98
• Concrete/brick: ε ≈ 0.85-0.95
• Ceramics: ε ≈ 0.75-0.90

Key Observation:
Shiny polished metals radiate poorly (low ε)
Rough/oxidized surfaces radiate well (high ε)
Opposite of visible light reflection!

KIRCHHOFF'S LAW:
Good absorbers are also good emitters
ε_radiator = α_absorber

Surface that looks dark (absorbs visible light) also radiates well (high ε)
Surface that looks shiny (reflects visible light) also radiates poorly (low ε)

Practical Implication:
Cannot improve cooling by polishing surface (reduces ε)
Painted/oxidized surface actually radiates better for cooling

RADIATION IN POWER PLANT ACCIDENTS:

Normal Operation Radiation Negligible:
• Primary coolant ≈ 550°F
• Convection dominates (h from forced flow ≈ 1000-2000)
• Radiation ≈ 1000-2000 Btu/(hr·ft²)
• Convection ≈ millions Btu/(hr·ft²) (much larger)
• Radiation is < 1% of total cooling

Loss of Coolant Scenario:
• Fuel cladding temperature rises rapidly (no convective cooling)
• At 1800-2000°F, radiation becomes significant
• At 2200°F+, radiation becomes major cooling pathway
• Zirconium-water reaction also occurs (adds heat, accelerates problem)

Effectiveness of Radiation Cooling:
At 2000°F with bare core:
Q_rad ≈ 44,400 Btu/(hr·ft²) (ε ≈ 0.8 oxidized zirconium)
This looks large, but compared to decay heat production:
• Early LOCA: Decay heat ≈ 6-8% of full power
• Full power equivalent ≈ ~400 MW for large reactor
• 6% ≈ 24 MW = 82 million Btu/h
• Fuel surface area ≈ 60,000 ft² (rough estimate for large core)
• Required heat flux = 82 million / 60,000 ≈ 1,400 Btu/(hr·ft²)
• Radiation available ≈ 44,400 Btu/(hr·ft²)

Result: Radiation CAN remove decay heat if cladding reaches critical temperature
But it's inadequate until temperatures very high (dangerous to cladding)
Goal: Maintain liquid cooling (convection) to prevent radiation cooling scenario

SINGLE-PHASE VS. TWO-PHASE HEAT TRANSFER:

SINGLE-PHASE HEAT TRANSFER:

Definition: Heat transfer where neither fluid changes phase.
Both fluids remain as pure liquid or pure gas throughout heat exchanger.

Examples:
• Heating air through air heater (air → air)
• Heating oil through tube (oil → oil, both liquid)
• Cooling water with cold water (water → water, both liquid)
• Condensate cooling (liquid → liquid cooling water)
• Not phase change, just temperature change

Heat Transfer Equation:
Q̇ = ṁ × cp × ΔT

Where:
• Q̇ = heat transfer rate [Btu/h]
• ṁ = mass flow rate [lbm/h]
• cp = specific heat capacity [Btu/(lbm·°F)]
• ΔT = temperature change [°F]

Energy Balance:
Hot stream loses enthalpy:
Q̇_lost = ṁ_hot × cp_hot × (T_hot,in - T_hot,out)

Cold stream gains enthalpy:
Q̇_gained = ṁ_cold × cp_cold × (T_cold,out - T_cold,in)

At equilibrium: Q̇_lost = Q̇_gained

Three Heat Transfer Layers in Series:
1. Convection from hot fluid to tube: Q̇ = h_hot × A_hot × ΔT
2. Conduction through tube wall: Q̇ = k × A_mean × ΔT / L
3. Convection from tube to cold fluid: Q̇ = h_cold × A_cold × ΔT

Combined:
Q̇ = U_o × A_o × ΔT_mean

Where U_o encompasses all three resistances

Overall Heat Transfer Coefficient:
1/U_o = 1/(h_hot × A_ratio) + L/(k × A_mean) + 1/h_cold

TWO-PHASE HEAT TRANSFER:

Definition: Heat transfer where one fluid changes phase during the process.

Examples:
1. BOILER (Steam Generator):
   • Hot side: Combustion gas (single-phase)
   • Cold side: Water → Steam (two-phase)
   • Phase change: Liquid water vaporizes to steam

2. CONDENSER:
   • Hot side: Steam → Liquid water (two-phase)
   • Cold side: Cooling water (single-phase)
   • Phase change: Steam condenses to liquid

3. FEEDWATER HEATERS:
   • Hot side: Extracted steam → Liquid condensate (two-phase)
   • Cold side: Feedwater heating (single-phase or two-phase in some designs)
   • Phase change: Steam condenses

Energy Balance for Two-Phase:
Q̇ = ṁ × Δh

Where:
• Δh = enthalpy change including latent heat [Btu/lbm]
• Example: h_liquid to h_steam (includes latent heat of vaporization)

Comparison to Single-Phase:
Single-phase: Q̇ = ṁ × cp × ΔT (sensible heat only)
Two-phase: Q̇ = ṁ × Δh (includes latent heat, much larger!)

Example - Boiler Comparison:

Single-phase scenario (only if no phase change):
ṁ = 100 lbm/s, cp = 1.0 Btu/(lbm·°F)
If ΔT = 100°F (increase water temp by 100°F only)
Q̇ = 100 × 1 × 100 = 10,000 Btu/s = 14.2 MW

Two-phase scenario (actual boiler):
ṁ = 100 lbm/s, Δh = 1200 Btu/lbm (liquid to steam)
Q̇ = 100 × 1200 = 120,000 Btu/s = 170 MW

Difference: 12× larger heat transfer for same mass flow!
This demonstrates why latent heat dominates boiler/condenser duty

WHY TWO-PHASE HEAT TRANSFER MORE EFFICIENT:

Latent Heat Much Larger than Sensible Heat:
Heat of vaporization for water: Δh_fg ≈ 700-1000 Btu/lbm
Temperature rise for same energy: ΔT = Δh_fg / cp = 700-1000 Btu/lbm / 1 Btu/(lbm·°F) = 700-1000°F

This huge temperature would be impractical, so two-phase process better

Convection Coefficient During Phase Change:
h during boiling or condensation ≈ 1000-10,000 Btu/(hr·ft²·°F)
Compare to: h for single-phase convection ≈ 100-1000 Btu/(hr·ft²·°F)
Increase of 10-100×!

Reason: Phase change bubbles/droplets create intense mixing
Bubbles scrape off boundary layer constantly
Thin effective thermal layer
Very high effective h

Process Comparison:
Sensible heating:
• Boundary layer develops slowly
• Linear temperature increase
• Moderate h

Boiling (latent heating):
• Bubbles form continuously (intense mixing)
• Phase change converts energy efficiently
• Very high h
• Small ΔT sufficient for large heat transfer

THREE RESISTANCES IN SERIES (Two-Phase):

Path: Hot fluid → Tube → Cold phase-changing fluid

1. Hot Side Convection:
If combustion gas cooling: h ≈ 20-50 (poor)
If steam: h ≈ 1000+ (excellent)

2. Tube Conduction:
k × L / A (usually small compared to convection resistance)

3. Cold Side Phase Change:
If boiling: h ≈ 1000-10,000 (excellent)
If condensing: h ≈ 1000-5000 (excellent)

Result:
Phase-change side (boiling or condensation) usually NOT limiting
Single-phase side usually dominates resistance

Example - Boiler:
h_combustion_gas ≈ 30 (dominant resistance!)
k and thickness: small
h_boiling: 2000+ (not limiting)

Design strategy: Improve combustion gas-side convection

Example - Condenser:
h_steam_condensing ≈ 2000+ (excellent, not limiting)
k and thickness: small
h_cooling_water ≈ 1000-2000 (usually limiting)

Design strategy: Increase cooling water flow to improve h_cold

CORE THERMAL POWER CALCULATION AND HEAT BALANCE:

DEFINITION:
Core Thermal Power (CTP) is the rate of thermal energy released by fission reactions in the reactor core. Also called "reactor thermal power" or "core power."

Units: MW (thermal), Btu/h, or fraction of rated power (%)

MEASUREMENT METHODS:

Method 1: PRIMARY SIDE HEAT BALANCE (RCS Heat Balance)
Heat removed from core by circulating coolant:

Q̇_primary = ṁ_RCS × cp × (T_h - T_c)

Where:
• ṁ_RCS = reactor coolant mass flow rate [lbm/s]
• cp = specific heat of primary coolant [Btu/(lbm·°F)] ≈ 1.0-1.1
• T_h = hot leg temperature (exiting core) [°F]
• T_c = cold leg temperature (entering core) [°F]
• Q̇_primary = thermal power [Btu/h]

Physical Meaning:
Coolant circulates through core
Absorbs heat equal to: mass flow × specific heat × temperature rise
All heat generated by fission absorbed by coolant
CTP = heat absorbed by primary coolant

Calculation:
Example:
• RCS flow: 150,000 lbm/h
• cp = 1.0 Btu/(lbm·°F)
• T_h = 600°F, T_c = 550°F (ΔT = 50°F)
• Q̇ = 150,000 × 1.0 × 50 = 7,500,000 Btu/h = 2,195 kW ≈ 2.2 MW (thermal)

Method 2: SECONDARY SIDE HEAT BALANCE (Boiler Side)
Heat transferred from primary to secondary through steam generator:

Q̇_secondary = ṁ_steam × (h_out - h_in)

Where:
• ṁ_steam = steam mass flow rate [lbm/s]
• h_out = steam outlet enthalpy [Btu/lbm] (from steam tables)
• h_in = feedwater inlet enthalpy [Btu/lbm] (from steam tables)
• Q̇_secondary = thermal power transferred to secondary [Btu/h]

Physical Meaning:
All heat from core transferred through steam generator
Secondary side receives enthalpy increase equal to: mass flow × enthalpy rise
At steady state: Q̇_primary ≈ Q̇_secondary

Calculation:
Example:
• Steam flow: 500 lbm/s = 1,800,000 lbm/h
• Inlet feedwater: 300°F, 2050 psia (h_in = 276 Btu/lbm)
• Outlet steam: 500 psia, 500°F (h_out = 1469 Btu/lbm)
• Q̇ = 1,800,000 × (1469 - 276) = 1,800,000 × 1193 = 2,147,000,000 Btu/h ≈ 2,147 MW (thermal)

COMPARISON AND DISCREPANCY ANALYSIS:

Ideal Case:
Q̇_primary = Q̇_secondary (perfect energy balance)

Reality:
Small differences due to:
• Pressurizer heater/cooler (maintains pressure, adds or removes small amount)
• RCP motor heat (motor driving pump adds small amount to fluid)
• Heat loss through insulation (very small, usually negligible)
• Measurement errors (instrumentation uncertainty)
• Blowdown from secondary (small amount of steam released)

Calculation:
Q̇_net = Q̇_primary - Q̇_secondary - Q̇_losses

If Q̇_net ≈ 0: System balanced
If Q̇_primary > Q̇_secondary: Energy unaccounted for (check losses)
If Q̇_secondary > Q̇_primary: Indicates measurement error or feedback effect

NUCLEAR INSTRUMENTATION:

Power Indication:
% Reactor Power = (Actual CTP) / (Rated CTP) × 100%

Example:
• Rated power: 3000 MW (thermal)
• Measured CTP: 2100 MW
• % Power = 2100 / 3000 × 100% = 70%

Advantages of Instrumentation:
• Direct measurement (not calculated)
• Fast response (doesn't require waiting for thermal equilibrium)
• Continuous during power changes

Disadvantages:
• Requires accurate calibration
• Sensor locations affect measurement
• Power distribution shape affects accuracy
• Must account for instrument drift

Comparison:
Instrumentation: Measures neutron flux (indicator of power) → fast but requires calibration
Heat balance: Calculates from temperatures → slower but independent verification

Typical Use:
During steady state: Both methods should agree
During power changes: Instruments respond faster, heat balance lags
Verify actual core power: Use heat balance to calibrate instruments

[Note ID: 21]
STEAM GENERATOR TUBE PLUGGING AND TEMPERATURE EFFECTS:

SCENARIO:
Some steam generator tubes have been plugged (isolated from service) due to corrosion/cracking.
Effect on system performance when power held constant.

GIVEN CONDITION:
• Original condition: 100% of tubes operational
• New condition: 2% of tubes plugged (cannot carry flow)
• Core power: HELD CONSTANT at same MW thermal

EFFECT ON U_o (Overall Heat Transfer Coefficient):
• Plugged tubes add thermal resistance? NO
• But remaining tubes have better flow distribution?
• Actually, U_o essentially unchanged (still 98% of original tubes active)
• More importantly: Total surface area (A) decreased by 2%

EQUATION ANALYSIS:
Q̇ = U_o × A × ΔT_mean

Same Q̇ (constant power)
A reduced by 2% (98% of original area)
U_o approximately unchanged (per unit area, quality of heat transfer similar)

Rearranging:
ΔT_mean = Q̇ / (U_o × A)
ΔT_mean_new / ΔT_mean_original = A_original / A_new = 1.00 / 0.98 ≈ 1.02

Temperature difference must increase 2% to maintain same heat transfer with 2% less area!

CALCULATION EXAMPLE:

Original Condition:
• Primary coolant: T_h = 600°F (hot leg), T_c = 550°F, ΔT_primary = 50°F
• Secondary: T_steam = 532°F, T_feedwater = 475°F, ΔT_secondary ≈ 57°F
• Average loop T: T_avg = (600 + 550) / 2 = 575°F
• Secondary steam generation: T_sat = 532°F @ system pressure
• Condenser temp: T_cond = 100°F
• T_avg_overall = (575 + 100) / 2 = 337.5°F (average of primary and condenser)

With 2% Plugging:
Required ΔT increased 2% to maintain same Q̇
New ΔT_primary = 50°F × 1.02 = 51°F

New temperatures (maintaining same core outlet T_h strategy):
If keeping power = flow × cp × ΔT
Same flow, same cp, slight increase in ΔT possible by:
• Increasing T_h (hotter outlet) by 1°F
• OR decreasing T_c (colder inlet) by 1°F
• OR combination

Typical operator action (maintaining thermal margin):
• Increase pressurizer temperature setpoint by 1-2°F
• This raises average primary temperature
• T_h increases, power maintained with slightly higher temp

Result:
New T_h = 601°F (1°F increase)
New T_c = 550°F (unchanged)
New ΔT = 51°F (2% increase)

Secondary Side Effect:
Steam generator now operates at:
• Same heat input (power held constant)
• Larger required temperature difference
• Higher primary inlet temperature (551°F for example)

New secondary:
• Inlet feedwater still ~475°F
• Steam outlet: Adjusts to satisfy heat balance
• T_sat_new might be 533°F (slight pressure increase due to higher T_h pushing more energy)

Average coolant temperature:
Original: T_avg = 575°F
New: T_avg = 575.5°F (slightly higher)

Plant Status:
• Power still constant (same MW thermal)
• Average temperature increased slightly
• System still operating normally
• Thermal margin to limits slightly reduced

LONG-TERM IMPLICATIONS:

Continued Tube Degradation:
More tubes plug over time
Every 2% reduction requires ΔT increase to maintain power
Eventually run out of margin:
• Reach maximum allowable pressurizer temperature
• Reach maximum allowable hot leg temperature
• Reach power uprate limit
• Plant cannot maintain power without exceeding limits

Action Required:
Before reaching limits: Perform steam generator maintenance
Options:
• Clean/inspect tubes (remove deposits)
• Plug additional degraded tubes
• Replace steam generator (major outage)
• Request power uprate reduction to lower average temperature

Efficiency Effect:
• Slight temperature increase (1-2°F) negligible for efficiency
• But demonstrates that tube degradation has real plant impact
• Cost: Lower effective heat transfer area → reduced plant output over time

THIS EXAMPLE SHOWS:
• How heat transfer coefficients and areas fundamentally limit power output
• Why steam generator condition critical to plant performance
• Interdependence of primary/secondary systems through heat transfer
• Real-time operating impacts of equipment degradation
