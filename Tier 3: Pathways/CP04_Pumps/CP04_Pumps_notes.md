# CP04_Pumps - Comprehensive Study Notes

## Note ID: 114
### Pumps are devices used for moving and/or altering the pressure of fluids in confined spaces using suction or pressure. The fundamental function of a pump is to move a given volume flow rate of fluid at a specific pressure from one level to another, while overcoming all flow resistance encountered between the fluid levels. Flow resistances can be caused by height differences, valves, piping, system geometry, fluid properties, and friction.



---

## Note ID: 115
### No matter the size, shape, or design of a pump, its basic purpose remains constant: to move fluids. However, "moving fluids" means different things in different situations. Moving water between points at the same level is relatively simple. But moving fluid from ground level to a tank 50 feet above requires significant pressure and demands more sophisticated pump designs.



---

## Note ID: 116
### Different pumps are designed to fulfill different functions. Some pumps are designed to move large volumes of fluid at relatively low pressure; others are made to pump small volumes at very high pressures. Some pumps handle thick, heavy fluids like fuel oil or slurry, while others are devoted to moving clear, light fluids like water, or gases like air. Despite these variations, all pumps share the same basic purpose: moving fluids through systems.



---

## Note ID: 117
### Pumps are classified into two main categories: Kinetic (dynamic) pumps and Positive displacement pumps. Kinetic pumps operate by converting mechanical energy into kinetic energy (velocity), then converting that velocity into pressure energy. Positive displacement pumps work by trapping a fixed volume of fluid and forcing it into the discharge line. Both categories include several different designs for different applications.



---

## Note ID: 118
### Kinetic or dynamic pumps use rotating impellers to impart velocity to the fluid. The fluid enters the pump at low velocity and low pressure, gains velocity from the impeller, then the pressure increases as the fluid exits. Common types include centrifugal pumps which are the most common in plant applications. These pumps are best for moving large volumes at moderate to low pressures.



---

## Note ID: 119
### Positive displacement pumps trap a fixed volume of fluid and force it through the discharge. As the displaced volume is replaced, a new volume is trapped and the cycle repeats. These pumps are best for moving small volumes at high pressures, or for moving viscous fluids. Types include gear pumps, screw pumps, piston pumps, and diaphragm pumps.



---

## Note ID: 120
### Capacity is a fundamental pump characteristic describing the amount of fluid a pump moves. Pump capacities are normally expressed in gallons per minute (gpm) or cubic feet per second (cfs). The amount moved for a given set of operating conditions is plotted on a pump operational curve (performance curve). The rated volumetric flow rate for a specific set of parameters is defined as the pump's capacity.



---

## Note ID: 121
### Static head is a difference in fluid levels (elevation head) and is normally stated in feet of water column. For example, if the discharge point is 25 feet higher than the suction point, there is a 25-foot static discharge head. Static head represents the pressure energy needed to lift fluid vertically against gravity and does not account for friction or flow velocity.



---

## Note ID: 122
### Dynamic head is the energy required to set the fluid in motion and to overcome any resistance to that motion. Dynamic head includes velocity head (kinetic energy of moving fluid) and friction head (energy lost to friction in pipes, fittings, and equipment). Dynamic head is also expressed in feet of water column. Total head is the sum of static head and dynamic head for a given condition.



---

## Note ID: 123
### Total suction head (Hs) is the amount of pressure, expressed in feet of water column, that is available at the suction of a pump. Total suction head equals the static suction head minus the dynamic suction head of the pump. When the suction source fluid level is above the pump centerline, suction head is positive. When below the pump centerline, the pump must "lift" the fluid, creating a negative suction head called "suction lift."



---

## Note ID: 124
### The equation for total suction head is: Hs = hs – hfs + ps, where Hs is total suction head, hs is static suction head, hfs is pressure drop in suction line, and ps is vacuum or pressure in a tank on the suction side. When the suction source is under vacuum or pressure, that pressure is included in the total suction head calculation. The dynamic head includes both friction losses and velocity head effects.



---

## Note ID: 125
### Total discharge head (Ht) is the sum of static discharge head and dynamic head of piping downstream of the pump, expressed in feet of water column. Static discharge head is the elevation difference between the pump centerline and the fluid level on the discharge side. If the pump discharges to a pressurized tank, the total discharge head includes that tank pressure. The discharge side dynamic head includes friction losses in discharge piping.



---

## Note ID: 126
### The equation for total discharge head is: Ht = ht + hft + pt, where Ht is total discharge head, ht is static discharge head, hft is pressure drop in discharge line, and pt is pressure in a tank on the discharge side. This equation accounts for both the elevation the fluid is raised (static) and the resistance to flow on the discharge side (dynamic). Accurate calculation requires knowledge of pipe diameters, lengths, and fitting losses.



---

## Note ID: 127
### Total head is the total pressure difference between total discharge head and total suction head of the pump. If pressure gauges are installed on the suction and discharge of the pump, the total head equals the difference in the two gauge readings. Total head can be calculated using the equation: H = Ht – (+Hs). Total head is also called total developed head or total dynamic head (TDH). This is the pressure rise the pump must produce.



---

## Note ID: 128
### Example calculation: Given a pump with suction source at 580 ft elevation, pump centerline at 592 ft, discharge destination at 617 ft, suction line pressure drop of 3 ft, and discharge line pressure drop of 6 ft. Total suction head = (580 – 592) – 3 = -15 ft. Total discharge head = (617 – 592) + 6 = 31 ft. Total head = 31 – (-15) = 46 ft. This total head represents what the pump must overcome to move fluid through the system.



---

## Note ID: 129
### Net positive suction head (NPSH) or available NPSH (NPSHA) is the absolute pressure at the suction of the pump minus the vapor pressure of the fluid at the same point. NPSH is critical for pump operation because it determines whether cavitation will occur. The required NPSH (NPSHR) is determined by pump design and supplied by the pump manufacturer. To prevent cavitation, available NPSH must be greater than required NPSH.



---

## Note ID: 130
### The NPSH equation is: NPSH = Psuction – Psat, where NPSH is net positive suction head, Psuction is absolute pressure at pump suction, and Psat is saturation pressure (vapor pressure) of the fluid. When NPSH equals zero, a partial vacuum forms in the liquid and the fluid begins to flash (boil) into vapor, risking cavitation. The saturation pressure varies with temperature; higher temperatures increase saturation pressure, reducing available NPSH.



---

## Note ID: 131
### Cavitation occurs when pump suction pressure approaches the saturation pressure of the fluid in the pump. Vapor bubbles begin forming at saturation pressure, causing the pump suction and casing to become vapor bound. Cavitation reduces fluid capacity in the pump and can cause noise, vibration, and severe pump damage. When cavitation occurs, capacity of the pump decreases and significant damage can occur quickly. Equipment damage from cavitation is one of the most serious operational failures.



---

## Note ID: 132
### Cavitation causes pitting and erosion of the pump impeller as vapor bubbles collapse or implode under high pressure at the discharge. This erosion can significantly reduce impeller efficiency and create imbalance. In addition to physical damage, cavitation results in flow oscillations, excessive pump vibration, pump overheating, and lower than normal flow rates. The reduction in capacity depends on the amount of vapor formed. Long-term cavitation can render a pump unusable.



---

## Note ID: 133
### Indicators of cavitation include distinctive noise that sounds like the pump is pumping rocks or has a solid object inside, vibration due to impeller imbalance from erosion, motor current fluctuations as flow varies, and fluctuations in discharge pressure and flow rates. Operators must be able to identify cavitation because early detection can prevent catastrophic pump failure. As cavitation occurs and damages the impeller, it cannot add the same kinetic energy, reducing pressure and flow.



---

## Note ID: 134
### Several methods prevent cavitation. Using a head tank to create static pressure at pump suction raises absolute pressure above saturation pressure. A booster pump can increase pressure at the suction of a main pump. Using large diameter suction piping converts kinetic energy to pressure energy, increasing pressure. Reducing system temperature where possible decreases saturation pressure. Reducing suction line restrictions and length minimizes friction losses. Proper system design and operational procedures are critical.



---

## Note ID: 135
### Cavitation prevention requires system preparation before pump startup. All air must be vented from suction lines because vapor bubbles and free air are much more compressible than liquid, leading to flow instability. Filling suction lines completely with fluid before starting the pump ensures liquid contact with the impeller. Operating procedures must include gentle startup to establish flow gradually, preventing sudden pressure fluctuations. Operators must monitor pump discharge pressure and flow patterns.



---

## Note ID: 136
### System design features for cavitation prevention include adequate suction tank sizing with adequate freeboard, proper water level control, temperature management to keep saturation pressure low, suction strainers with low pressure drop, short and large-diameter suction piping to minimize friction losses, and proper valve placement. In closed systems, maintenance of air removal during startup is essential. In open systems, atmospheric pressure aids in preventing cavitation. The relationship between temperature and saturation pressure must be understood.



---

## Note ID: 137
### Pump operational curves show the relationship between flow rate and the head (pressure) a pump can develop. These curves are provided by manufacturers and show pump performance at different operating points. The pump's operating point occurs where the pump curve intersects the system resistance curve (sometimes called the system head curve). Understanding pump curves helps select appropriate pumps and predict performance at different operating conditions. Curves vary significantly between different pump types and designs.



---

## Note ID: 138
### Pump efficiency is a measure of how effectively a pump converts input mechanical power to useful hydraulic power. Efficiency decreases as flow rate deviates from the pump's design point. Most pumps operate at peak efficiency in a specific range of flow rates and heads. Operating a pump outside this range wastes energy as heat, accelerates wear, and can cause cavitation. Selection of an appropriately sized pump is important for plant efficiency and reliability. Part-load operation reduces efficiency significantly for many pump types.



---

## Note ID: 139
### Pump performance depends on several factors including system design, fluid properties, temperature effects, and pump condition. Water properties like density and viscosity affect pump performance. Temperature affects fluid viscosity and saturation pressure. As temperature increases, viscosity decreases and saturation pressure increases, both affecting pump operation. Fluid contamination and degradation affect viscosity and can cause erosion of internal pump components. Regular system maintenance including filtration keeps fluid properties stable and protects pump life.



---

## Note ID: 140
### Centrifugal pump impellers can be designed in different configurations to handle different fluid types and operating conditions. Radial flow impellers are the most common and best for low-viscosity fluids. Axial flow (propeller) impellers are designed for high-flow, low-pressure applications. Mixed-flow impellers combine radial and axial characteristics. Special impeller designs handle abrasive slurries or liquids with gas content. Understanding impeller type helps operators recognize pump capabilities and limitations in their plant systems.



---

## Note ID: 141
### Positive displacement pump operation is fundamentally different from centrifugal pumps. These pumps deliver a fixed volume of fluid with each cycle, regardless of discharge pressure (up to their designed maximum). This makes them excellent for moving high-viscosity fluids or when constant flow is required. However, they are more complex, more expensive, have higher maintenance requirements, and are more susceptible to cavitation. Gear pumps, screw pumps, and piston pumps are common types, each with distinct advantages and limitations.



---

## Note ID: 142
### In plant systems, pump reliability and availability are critical for operations. Understanding pump theory helps operators recognize abnormal conditions early and take corrective action. Common problems include cavitation, seal leakage, bearing wear, vibration, and performance degradation. Preventive maintenance includes regular inspection, vibration monitoring, thermal monitoring, and fluid analysis. Operators trained in pump theory can better diagnose problems and work with maintenance personnel to ensure pumps operate reliably and efficiently throughout their service life.



---

## Note ID: 143
### Summary of key pump concepts: Pumps move fluids by overcoming system resistance. Capacity is flow rate; total head is pressure rise required. Static head is elevation difference; dynamic head is friction and velocity effects. Suction head can be positive (above pump) or negative (below pump, called lift). Cavitation occurs when suction pressure drops to saturation pressure, causing vapor bubble formation and equipment damage. Prevention requires maintaining adequate NPSH through system design and operational procedures. Understanding pump theory is essential for safe, reliable operation.
