---
As you walk through a power plant, the most noticeable feature besides the large equipment is the enormous maze of piping and valves -- miles of piping and valves of numerous sizes and shapes. This lesson covers the requirements and considerations used when designing piping systems and the devices used to support and align piping, plus the common types of valves in the power plant: their function, principles of operation, and the methods used to determine whether they are open, closed, or in an intermediate position.

section: 2.1 PURPOSE OF PIPING
objective: Objective 1
---
Water and steam are the two main fluids used in power generating plants to transport energy from one piece of equipment to another. Piping connects this equipment and provides a means of containing the fluids as they flow throughout the plant. The purpose of piping is to provide housing through which fluids -- and in some cases mixtures of fluids and solid matter -- are transported from one place to another.

id: 146
section: 2.2 PIPING DESIGN FACTORS
objective: Objective 1
---
Piping systems are designed to provide safe and efficient plant operation. Based on the following design factors, the appropriate pipe size, wall thickness, strength, and material are chosen: Fluid Temperature (hot or cold, and the range it varies over); Fluid Pressure (adds loading stress beyond the mass of the fluid itself); Fluid Volume/Mass Flow Rate (higher velocity can erode the inner wall of the piping); Chemical Properties of the Fluid (corrosiveness, system purity requirements); Physical Properties of the Fluid (mass, viscosity, molecule size); Fluid Contamination (e.g. radioactive material); Shock Loads (water hammer, steam hammer, thermal shock, earthquake); and Changes in Direction (flow energy at a change in direction, like an elbow, can cause erosion and wall-thickness reduction).

---
id: 147
section: 2.3 PIPING MATERIALS
objective: Objective 2
---
A variety of piping materials are used to get the best material for the lowest cost, since plant fluids vary in chemical composition, temperature, and pressure. Low-Carbon Steel: rusts easily but low cost and easy to work with; used for water, low temperature steam, air, and fuel oil. Low-Alloy Steel: can rust and costs/works harder than low-carbon steel, but has greater resistance to wear, fatigue, and corrosion; used in high temperature situations. Stainless Steel: corrosion resistant; used where production of corrosion products is a concern, such as systems that meet the reactor. Copper Alloys (brass, bronze): excellent corrosion resistance, electrical/thermal conductivity, good workability; used in low/moderate temperature applications (copper tubing, brass fittings, electrical conductors). Nickel Alloys (Monel, Inconel): excellent corrosion resistance, used in high temperature/pressure equipment (heat exchangers, steam generators, condensers); high cost. Plastic: high corrosion resistance, light weight, easily joined; used for water/drainage systems and chemicals. Other specialized materials include cast iron, concrete, and fiberglass.

section: 2.4.1 PIPING SUPPORTS, RESTRAINTS, AND SNUBBERS
objective: Objective 3
---
A piping support or restraint is any device designed to support a load (piping) and/or control its movement, while still allowing the pipe to make whatever movement is needed to minimize stress on the system. Piping loads can result from the fluid conveyed, pressure, gravity, thermal expansion, or other causes. There is a wide variety of supports/restraints: rigid restraints support the pipe in at least one direction; spring hangers act vertically during the gravity loading condition; snubbers limit seismic motion while allowing thermal growth; and there are many other types.

---
id: 149
section: 2.4.1 PRECAUTIONS FOR PIPING SUPPORTS AND RESTRAINTS
objective: Objective 4
---
Piping supports/restraints contribute significantly to the safe and proper performance of the piping system and are also expensive to replace. Unless operating under the direction of a procedure/work request, do NOT: climb on them; attach any rigging, valve chains, etc. to them; adjust them in any way; or remove them for any reason. Undue stress on pipe joints, fittings, and components can gradually cause system degradation or catastrophic failure during seismic events, severe transients, or water hammer -- especially on hazardous systems like steam and acid lines.

---
id: 150
section: 2.4.2 THERMAL EFFECTS ON PIPING
objective: Objective 5
---
Due to high plant temperatures, piping must be designed to expand freely; without an arrangement for thermal expansion, equipment damage or ruptured piping can result. Devices placed in lines to compensate for thermally-induced forces include: Thermal Loops (loops/bends designed to allow thermal linear expansion and contraction); Piping Expansion Joints (protect piping from thermal linear expansion); and Thermal Insulation (improves efficiency and reduces heat loss, and adds safety by protecting workers from hot surfaces -- do not disturb insulation that may contain asbestos, and report any loose or damaged insulation).

section: 3.1 PURPOSE OF VALVES
objective: Objective 6
---
Valves are devices that control the flow of fluids, performing functions such as: starting and stopping flow, throttling flow, controlling the direction of flow, regulating pressure, and relieving pressure. Each type performs best when used as intended. Although designs vary greatly, all valves share similar characteristics: opening a valve increases flow and decreases differential pressure (D/P) or headloss across it; closing a valve increases D/P, backpressure, and headloss while decreasing flow and downstream pressure.

---
id: 152
section: 3.2 BASIC VALVE CONSTRUCTION - MAIN COMPONENTS
objective: Objectives 7/8
---
The basic parts of a valve, regardless of type, are the body, bonnet, disk, seat rings, and stem. The Body is the main element -- it directs flow through the valve, past the disk and its mating surface (the seat); bodies are cast or forged and machined at interfaces with the bonnet, seat rings, and piping connections. The Bonnet mates with the body and supports the stem, disk, and actuator; it is cast or forged, connected by threaded/bolted/welded joints, and houses the packing that seals between bonnet and stem. The Disk controls flow -- as it closes it restricts (throttles) flow until fully shut; throttling increases D/P and reduces downstream pressure/flow. Seat Rings provide the seating surface for the disk (some designs machine the body itself as the seat). The Stem attaches to the disk and extends out of the valve so an outside force can position the disk, typically via T-heads, threaded fittings, or flexible joints.

---
id: 153
section: 3.2 VALVE STEM DETAILS, PACKING, AND RISING/NON-RISING DESIGN
objective: Objectives 7/8
---
Above the disk connection, the stem may have a wider, beveled area called the backseat, which mates with a similarly beveled bonnet surface. Not all valves have backseats. The backseat's primary purpose is to remove system pressure from the packing if leakage is excessive and adjustment can't correct it, and it allows packing replacement at normal system pressure (though this is not normally done). Backseating an MOV can result in the valve being unable to close on demand; backseating of safety-related valves is documented and carefully controlled. Valves are classified as rising stem (the stem is threaded into a stationary surface -- yoke bushing or actuator stem nut -- and rises as it's turned) or non-rising stem (the stem is threaded into a sleeve connected to the disk; the sleeve and disk rise but the stem does not). The actuator operates the stem/disk assembly and may be a manual handwheel/lever, motor operator, pneumatic operator, solenoid operator, or hydraulic ram. Where the stem penetrates the pressure boundary, a seal (commonly compression-type packing, in materials like asbestos, Teflon, graphite, or metal) is required to prevent leakage -- a particular concern in a nuclear plant due to radiological effects. The packing sits in the stuffing box; a packing gland on top can be tightened to compress the packing outward against both the stuffing box walls and the stem. Both the stuffing box and stem must have a smooth, clean finish to seal properly; overtightened packing increases friction and stroking force and can cause stem binding.

section: 4.1.1 GATE VALVE CONSTRUCTION AND USES
objective: Objectives 9A/10A
---
Gate valves start or stop flow but are not intended to regulate or throttle. The disk is completely removed from the flow stream when fully open, giving virtually no resistance and little pressure drop when open, and a good seal when fully closed. This low delta-P when open is an advantage. Gate valves are used almost exclusively as isolation valves -- normally either fully open or fully closed. Positioning the disk partway in the flow stream causes turbulence that accelerates wear, so gate valves make poor throttle valves and can be damaged if misused (a disadvantage). Disks may be parallel (seating surfaces perpendicular to flow) or wedge design (converging angles to the flow path; may be solid, split, or flexible) -- wedge disks usually seal both inlet and outlet ports when closed. Guides (a ridge in the body fitting a groove in the disk) align the disk with the seat until the valve is almost shut; as the valve ages, gate-to-guide wear increases clearance and can cause misalignment, excess friction, and gouging that prevents proper sealing. Gate valves are adaptable to any actuator type, but the actuator must overcome the friction from D/P and disk/seat/guide contact at maximum design differential pressure. A double, parallel, or sliding disk gate valve uses two independent disk parts separated by opposing wedges; stem force pushes the wedges apart, sliding the disks outward to seal against both seats.

---
id: 155
section: 4.1.2 GATE VALVE SYSTEM FLOW VERSUS POSITION
objective: Objectives 9A/10A
---
Head loss in a system results from frictional losses in piping, valves, and other components. Because throttle valves are commonly sized based on pipe diameter, the pressure loss through a typical gate valve only significantly affects system pressure loss (and flow) during roughly the first 50% of opening; from ~50% to 100% open, the pressure loss across the valve seat is insignificant compared to other system losses. In practice, the first 25% of opening produces significantly more change in system flow rate than the last 25% of opening (75-100%).

section: 4.2 GLOBE VALVES
objective: Objectives 9B/10B
---
Globe valves (named for the resemblance of the body to a globe) are used to stop, start, and regulate fluid flow. The seat is perpendicular to flow or at a sharp angle, creating a tortuous flow path and a high pressure loss across the valve -- even when fully open. The disk can be raised completely clear of the flow path or completely close it; with proper mating, little or no leakage occurs when closed. The disk is beveled, giving globe valves good throttling ability, so they're used for both start/stop and flow-regulating functions. It's generally easy to get very low seat leakage because the disk is driven into the seat at nearly right angles, letting the closing force seat the disk tightly. Globe valves can be arranged so the disk closes with or against the direction of flow: closing against flow means fluid impedes closing but aids opening; closing with flow means fluid aids closing but impedes opening. The preferred flow direction is up through the seat, which reduces disk vibration and removes pressure from the stem packing when closed.

id: 157
section: 4.3 KEROTEST VALVES
objective: Objectives 9C/10C
---
The Kerotest valve is a unique, "pack-less" type of globe valve that uses a series of diaphragms as the pressure boundary, sealing the bonnet from the body and preventing leakage around the stem. The yoke screws into the body, sealing tightly to the disks; below the disks is a wet surface, above is dry. Four packing rings in the bonnet back up the diaphragm arrangement, and Kerotest valves also have backseats. The valve can go from full open to full closed in as little as 1 1/4 turns when properly adjusted, giving a large flow change with very little movement -- but excessive closing torque can crush (deform) the disk and prevent the valve from opening. The seat is spring-loaded open and pressure-closed; closing pressure transmits from the stem through the diaphragms to the spring-loaded seat. Flow direction is bottom to top over the seat, so the fluid must be free of abrasives/foreign material -- the seat-to-disk opening is only about 1/8". If foreign material is suspected of preventing a tight seal, the valve should be flushed by re-opening and re-closing. These valves are NOT designed for reverse flow -- the small clearance can cause severe vibration/chattering that may fail nearby welds. Kerotest valves are generally used on 2" or smaller lines in safety-related systems where leakage is unacceptable.

id: 159
section: 4.4 NEEDLE VALVES
objective: Objectives 9D/10D
---
Needle valves make fine adjustments to fluid flow. They are basically modified globe valves with a long, narrow, cone-shaped point on the stem end serving as the disk, permitting a very gradual change in opening size -- more precise throttling than a standard globe valve. They're used where precise flow control is desired, such as sampling lines. Because of their precise, delicate nature and the large disk-to-seat contact area (which reduces sealing force), needle valves are poor isolation valves and are not usually used for that purpose; another valve in line is typically used to stop/start flow after the needle valve is set.

---
id: 160
section: 4.5 BUTTERFLY VALVES
objective: Objectives 9E/10E
---
Butterfly valves stop, start, and regulate fluid flow in high-flow, large-diameter, low-pressure systems such as circulating water. They typically use a soft, resilient seat (rubber or neoprene); the stem extends through the disk, letting it rotate on an axis through its centerline, at a right angle to flow when open. Fully open, they exhibit little flow resistance and minimal pressure loss; fully closed, little seat leakage occurs if the resilient seat is in good condition. At intermediate positions, throttling occurs, though flow-regulating characteristics are not as good as a globe valve's. Over-torquing can cause the disk to travel past the seat, immediately causing high leakage, and can also cause permanent seat damage when the valve is opened.

---
id: 161
section: 4.6 PLUG AND BALL VALVES
objective: Objectives 9F/10F/9G/10G
---
Plug and ball valves are used only to stop or start fluid flow, named for the shape of their disk. A plug valve's body is machined to receive a tapered or cylindrical plug with a bored passage; in the open position the passage lines up with the inlet/outlet ports, and turning the plug 90 degrees blocks the ports with solid plug material. Ball valves work similarly but use a solid ball with a bored passage as the disk. Both offer little resistance to operation and are relatively quick-acting; differential pressure across the open valve is minimal and has little effect on closing torque. Ball valves are usually oriented so the handle is 90 degrees to the pipe when closed and in line with the pipe when open. Both can be designed for any system pressure; their primary disadvantages are (1) cost, (2) relatively rapid wear/corrosion of the plug-to-body or ball-to-body seats, and (3) inability to regulate flow.

---
id: 162
section: 4.7 DIAPHRAGM VALVES
objective: Objectives 9H/10H
---
Diaphragm valves start, regulate, and stop fluid flow, consisting of a body, bonnet, diaphragm, compressor, and stem. They resemble a globe valve in basic construction and seating area; the flexible diaphragm acts as the disk, and the compressor (attached to the stem) pushes the diaphragm against the seat to close flow. The bonnet surrounds the working parts and, like the diaphragm, bolts to the body. Diaphragm valves are not used for high pressure or high temperature, but mainly with radioactive or corrosive liquids/gases -- since the fluid is completely separated from the moving parts, they are not subject to contamination or degeneration, one of their key advantages. They offer long-lasting reliability, zero stem leakage, unrestricted streamlined flow with minimum pressure loss, precise throttling, and are vacuum-tight for both in-leakage and seat leakage.

id: 163
section: 4.8 CHECK VALVES
objective: Objectives 9I/10I
---
Check valves permit flow in only one direction; they close when downstream pressure exceeds upstream pressure, blocking backflow. There are three basic types, any of which can be designed for any system pressure. Swing Check: a hinged disk swings open or shut based on flow direction -- flow inlet-to-outlet swings it open, flow outlet-to-inlet closes it with positive seating force; offers little resistance when open, used where open-direction pressure loss must be minimized. Lift Check: body resembles a globe valve, causing a relatively large pressure loss due to flow direction changes; the disk is guided by slide rails and a short, fixed stem, lifting off the seat with inlet-to-outlet flow and seating on reverse flow -- typically less reverse-flow leakage than a swing check. Stop Check: more closely resembles a globe valve, similar in construction to a lift check, but the stem extends out to an actuator so it can be forcibly closed in both directions, combining check and globe valve functions; when the actuator inserts the stem it seats the disk (stopping flow), and when withdrawn the disk is free to lift/close by flow direction like a lift check.

section: 4.9.1 SAFETY AND RELIEF VALVE APPLICATION AND CONSTRUCTION
objective: Objectives 9J/10J/9K/10K
---
A relief valve's primary purpose is to maintain system integrity by preventing component/system overpressure, opening to relieve excess pressure once system pressure reaches its preset setpoint, opening further as pressure rises until fully open (relieving at maximum rated capacity), and closing once pressure drops below the setpoint. It's held closed by spring tension; overpressure overcomes the spring force and lifts the disk. Accumulation is the pressure above the preset setpoint at which the valve is fully open, expressed as a percent of the preset pressure. Safety valves are similar in function (sometimes called safety relief valves) but provide a large-volume release path at low overpressure, designed to fully open at the setpoint with little or no accumulation, staying full open until pressure falls below setpoint and then closing sharply. Blowdown is the difference between the preset setpoint and the actual reseating pressure, expressed as a percent of setpoint. The key difference between safety and relief valves is the quick opening action (little/no accumulation) of the safety valve versus the accumulation of the relief valve. Because mass flow rate/loss is lower for steam than water, and pressure drop is faster when a water-filled vessel's valve lifts versus one passing steam, relief valves are mainly used on liquid systems while safety valves are mainly used on steam/gas systems. Power Operated Relief Valves (PORVs) on the pressurizer vapor space relieve steam (not water) for primary system overpressure protection, allowing rapid energy release with less coolant inventory loss than valves relieving water. For high-pressure testing (e.g. a hydrostatic test above the relief setting), a gagging device can screw through the bonnet to lock the stem down, preventing the valve from lifting during the test.

id: 165
section: 4.9.2 SAFETY VS. RELIEF VALVE PERFORMANCE
objective: Objectives 9J/10J/9K/10K
---
A relief valve starts opening once the closure spring force is reached by rising system pressure, continuing to open as pressure rises until fully open (accumulation); past that point, flow increases more slowly as the D/P across the valve increases with further pressure rise. As pressure decreases, flow decreases along the same trace. A safety valve responds very differently: it "pops" open at the setpoint with little or no accumulation needed to reach fully open; if pressure continues rising, flow continues increasing as D/P rises; as pressure decreases, the valve will not close until pressure is clearly below the lift setpoint (blowdown). Protecting a tank with a relief valve: pressure rises to the lift setpoint and continues rising (accumulation) until mass flow into the tank equals mass flow out of the valve. Protecting the same tank with a safety valve: pressure rises to the lift setpoint, then quickly drops below setpoint (blowdown) as the valve pops open and reseats; the cycle repeats as long as flow keeps entering the tank.

section: 4.9.3 CALCULATING RELIEF PRESSURE, ACCUMULATION, AND BLOWDOWN
objective: Objectives 9J/10J/9K/10K
---
Relief pressure can be calculated from spring force and disk area, since pressure equals force divided by area. Example: a spring applies 1200 lbf to a disk 3 inches in diameter (area = 7.07 in^2); opening pressure = 1200 lbf / 7.07 in^2 = 170 psi. Accumulation is the pressure above the preset pressure at which the valve is fully open, as a percent of preset pressure. Example: setpoint 1,200 psia, fully open at 1,242 psia; accumulation = 1242 - 1200 = 42 psia, or (42/1200) x 100% = 3.5%. Blowdown is the difference between the preset relief setpoint and the actual reseating pressure, as a percent of preset pressure. Example: a safety valve opens at 1200 psi and closes at 1140 psi; blowdown = 1200 - 1140 = 60 psi, or (60/1200) x 100% = 5%.

section: 4.10 VACUUM BREAKERS
objective: Objective 9L
---
A vacuum breaker acts as the opposite of a pressure relief device: while relief/safety valves protect vessels from rupturing due to over-pressure, vacuum breakers protect tanks or vessels from collapsing due to low-pressure (vacuum) conditions. Typically, a spring-loaded valve opens to admit ambient pressure from outside the tank, increasing internal pressure and preventing collapse.

section: 4.11 STEAM TRAPS
objective: Objectives 9M/10M
---
Steam traps are installed in steam lines to remove condensate while preventing loss of steam -- especially important to avoid water carrying into the main turbine. Most consist of a valve plus a device that opens or closes it to drain condensate without letting steam escape. Bellows-Type: expansion of a volatile liquid's vapor in a sealed bellows closes the valve as steam heats it; as steam cools and condenses, the bellows contracts, opening the valve to drain condensate. Thermostatic Traps: generally more compact with fewer moving parts than mechanical traps. Impulse-Type: relies on hot water under pressure flashing to steam when pressure is reduced; the disk (with a flange acting as a piston, and a control orifice through it) balances forces above and below the flange -- as condensate approaches its boiling point, control flow flashes to steam above the flange, building pressure that closes the valve once it reaches about 86% of inlet pressure; as the condensate cools, pressure in that chamber drops and the valve reopens, repeating continuously. With a normal condensate load, the valve opens/closes frequently in small discharges; with a heavy load, it stays open for continuous discharge. Bucket-Type: as condensate enters, the bucket floats and the valve (connected to the bucket) closes; when the bucket fills and sinks, the valve opens and drains until the bucket floats again and re-closes the valve.

section: 5.0/5.1 VALVE ACTUATORS AND FAILURE POSITIONS
objective: Objectives 12A/12B/12C/12D/12E
---
A valve actuator manipulates the position of the valve stem. There are four basic types: mechanical (manual), electrical, pneumatic, and hydraulic -- the type used depends on factors like valve type/size, required operating thrust, and desired behavior on a failure. For actuators other than manual, it's important to know the position a valve will go to when the actuating medium (air, electrical power, hydraulic pressure) is lost: Fail Open -- pneumatic/hydraulic actuators can be designed so spring force strokes the valve fully open on loss of air/oil pressure, when that's the desired safe state. Fail Closed -- the actuator is instead configured so spring force drives the valve fully closed on loss of pressure. Fail As Is -- the valve remains in its current position if motive force is lost; most often associated with electrical actuators, specifically motor operated valves (MOVs), which must be manually repositioned if power to the motor is unavailable. Fail Safe -- the term for the position a valve must go to in order to protect the system or equipment on loss of signal; it determines whether the valve is designed to fail open or fail closed.

section: 5.2 MANUAL ACTUATORS
objective: Objective 11, 13A
---
Manual (mechanical) actuators consist of a handwheel or lever that directly controls stem position; remote reach rods are sometimes used when environmental conditions prevent local operation. Some large valves use a gearing arrangement to give the handwheel mechanical advantage; however, a mechanical actuator is typically not acceptable where rapid response time is required.

---
id: 171
section: 5.3 ELECTRIC ACTUATORS - MOVs AND SOLENOIDS
objective: Objective 13B
---
Valves with electric actuators fall into two categories: Motor Operated Valves (MOVs) and Solenoid Operated Valves. MOVs can be remotely or locally (manual handwheel) operated; the motor is typically high-speed with a gear reduction assembly to increase torque. On a power failure, an MOV fails "as is" since operating torque is removed, so further movement requires manual operation. Care must be taken during manual operation to avoid excessive seating/backseating torque (normally prevented by torque and limit switches) -- the torque needed to break the valve free may exceed available motor torque, so it's good practice to tag out power to the motor before manual operation, when feasible. A declutch lever engages/disengages the valve stem from the motor or handwheel; it should never be operated while the motor is running, to avoid damaging the clutch mechanism, and it automatically disengages the handwheel and re-engages the motor when the motor is actuated after manual operation. As a result of the Crystal River event, using valve wrenches to manually operate MOVs is prohibited. Solenoid valves use a magnetic core attached to the stem, attracted by the coil's magnetic force when energized; they're typically fully open or fully closed, with failure position (on loss of power to the coil) determined by spring placement.

---
id: 172
section: 5.4 PNEUMATIC ACTUATORS
objective: Objective 13C
---
Pneumatic actuators use gas pressure (air, nitrogen, etc.) as thrust to operate the valve stem. There are four basic types: Gas Cylinder (a piston under gas pressure drives the valve; designed with a fail-safe position via spring action on loss of pressure); Gas Piston (similar to the cylinder but with a shorter stroke -- typically 1 3/4" to 8" versus 9" to 24" for the cylinder -- and can also provide fail-safe); Gas Diaphragm (a resilient diaphragm forms a pressure-tight compartment opposed by spring action, with the stem attached to the diaphragm); and Air Vane (designed for rotary/quarter-turn valves, directly mountable to the stem without gearing, lightweight with few moving parts, and can provide fail-safe operation in several designs). To determine how a pneumatic valve will fail on loss of power: locate the gas solenoid valve in the supply line (labeled fail open/FO or fail closed/FC, shown de-energized on diagrams), then determine which direction the spring will drive the valve if gas is lost.

---
id: 173
section: 5.5 HYDRAULIC ACTUATORS
objective: Objective 13D
---
Hydraulic actuators use a piston to translate a signal into valve motion; hydraulic fluid is typically ported to one side of the piston, with the other side used as a return line or spring-loaded. The force required for valve operation can be varied by changing cylinder size or system pressure. Hydraulic actuators also have a fail-safe position, directed by spring action, when hydraulic pressure is lost.

section: 5.6 VALVE CONTROLLER OPERATION
objective: Objectives 13A/13B/13C/13D
---
A valve controller can automatically or manually position a valve from a remote location. In automatic mode, the controller senses a change in a system parameter (like pressure or temperature) and repositions the valve to restore it. In manual mode, the operator has remote control and the valve no longer responds to system parameter changes -- the operator must adjust the controller directly to maintain system parameters.

section: 6.1/6.2 VALVE POSITION VERIFICATION - LIGHT INDICATION AND TRAVEL INDICATION
objective: Objectives 13/14/15
---
Correctly determining valve position by inspection is essential for safe operation. The simplest method is observing the position-indicating light: limit switches attached to the valve stem drive remote and/or local lights -- a red light indicates open, a green light indicates closed, and both lights lit typically indicate mid-position (throttled, or being stroked open/closed). Another method is locally observing valve stem position -- e.g. on a rising stem valve, the handwheel stays stationary while the stem moves in/out of the bonnet as the valve closes/opens. A locally mounted pointer, mechanically connected to the stem, can also indicate position against a plate. Some valves have no visual indication at all, requiring system parameters (flow, pressure, temperature) or an attempted operation instead. When locally determining position, manual operation should only be attempted in the closed direction until stem movement is observed; if the valve was open, the stem will move closed (confirming it was open), and it should then be returned to its previous open position. If the stem doesn't move with nominal closing torque, the valve should be considered closed -- an open, mechanically bound valve can appear closed since the handwheel won't move in the close direction; if bound, use downstream system parameters to confirm actual position. Checking a manual valve by moving it a quarter-turn closed is used because it has negligible system effects either way.

id: 176
section: 6.3 SYSTEM INDICATION METHODS
objective: Objectives 13/14/15
---
Several additional methods confirm valve position. Senses: touch, sound, or sight can confirm a response to a change in valve position. Correct Number of Handwheel Turns: used for throttle valves, verified against a known number of turns open/closed. Visible Stem: normally used for rising stem gate valves, where the stem's position changes visibly as the disk opens/closes. Verifying a Valve is Shut: attempt to move it further closed -- if no movement occurs, it's probably shut (but confirm with other methods too, and avoid excessive force). Verifying a Valve is Open: attempt to move it in the closed direction -- if movement occurs, it was open and must be returned to fully open; never move it open to check it open, since that would initiate unwanted flow if it was actually closed. Checking Butterfly/Plug Valves: observe the operating lever -- parallel to the piping means open, perpendicular means closed.

section: 6.5 VALVE FAILURE MECHANISMS
objective: Objectives 11/17/18
---
Manual handwheel operation is usually counterclockwise to open and clockwise to shut. Operators should use both hands on a handwheel to control lateral forces and prevent bending the stem; if unable to operate a valve with nominal torque, an approved valve wrench should be used to free it -- never cheater bars or multiple operators, which can cause excessive stem torque. Thermal Binding: because some valve bodies cool faster than the disk, the seat can bind against the disk; when a valve is closed hot and allowed to cool, differential thermal contraction can bind the seats so tightly that reopening is impossible until the body is reheated. To prevent this during cooldown, affected valves can be cracked open and re-shut periodically, and using proper closing torque helps prevent binding. Pressure (Bonnet) Locking: liquid trapped in the valve bonnet can pressurize as it heats, and without a relief path (via packing or disk/seat leakage), the pressure buildup can prevent valve operation; this can be prevented with a relief/vent valve on the bonnet. Valve sticking can also result from overtightened packing causing stem binding. Some remotely controlled valves cannot be manually operated without switch repositioning or override, and may reposition automatically if manual operation is attempted; electrically interlocked valve circuits mean operating one valve may affect other components -- P&IDs and approved procedures minimize this risk. Ambient temperature and humidity variations can affect position-indication instrumentation accuracy/reliability (resistance changes, moisture-caused short circuits/grounds/corrosion), minimized by maintaining proper environmental conditions. SOER 84-7 case: multiple gate valves failed to open due to disk binding from thermal binding or pressure locking (trapped high-pressure water in the bonnet cavity without a vent path), a potential common-mode failure mechanism for safety-related systems -- in one case, three of four reactor depressurization system isolation valves failed to open during surveillance testing; in another, an RHR suction isolation valve could not be opened by motor or manual operation until insulation was removed and the valve cooled. Two principal gate valve seat designs are used in safety systems: wedge (flexible wedge is most common, with hollowed-out areas behind the seating surfaces for alignment flexibility) and double-disc parallel-seat (upstream pressure assists closure as in all gate valves, plus additional seating force from the actuator's closing motion forcing two disc parts outward against parallel seats via a wedge mechanism).
