# CP06_Heat_Exchangers - Comprehensive Study Notes

## Note ID: 69
### **SECTION 1.0 - INTRODUCTION**

Heat exchangers are particularly important in power plant operation. A heat exchanger is a device designed to transfer heat from one fluid to another. Two key concepts: (1) Heat can only be transferred when there is a temperature difference between substances; (2) Heat always transfers from higher temperature substance to lower temperature substance. Heat is transferred primarily by conduction (heat transfer between adjacent molecules through material) and convection (heat transfer by bulk motion and mixing of fluid portions).

---

## Note ID: 70
### **SECTION 2.0 - TYPES OF HEAT EXCHANGERS**

**OBJECTIVE 1: Explain the difference between direct contact and indirect contact (surface) heat exchangers.**
Two general types of heat exchangers: Direct contact heat exchangers - the two fluids come in contact with each other (examples: cooling towers, lakes, rivers). Indirect contact (surface) heat exchangers - the two fluids are separated by a solid surface such as a tube or metal plate (examples: steam generators, main condensers). Direct contact is less common; indirect contact more commonly used in power plant applications.

---

## Note ID: 71
### **SECTION 2.1 - COOLING TOWERS**

**OBJECTIVE 2: Describe the principles of operations of Forced Draft and Natural Draft Cooling Towers.**
Cooling towers exchange energy from cooling water into air flowing through the tower using evaporative cooling. The cooled circulating water serves as heat sink for main condensers. Two main types: open-circuit and closed-circuit. Two basic flow configurations: Cross-flow (air enters side, flows across, exits opposite side) and Counter flow (air enters bottom, flows upward; water enters top, flows downward). Towers may be forced-draft (fans blow air in), induced-draft (fans draw air through), or natural-draft (uses convective chimney effects).

---

## Note ID: 72
### **COOLING TOWER DESIGN AND FILL MATERIAL**

Tower fill material traditionally redwood but now mostly PVC or plastic. Critical factors for efficiency: (1) effectiveness of spray or splash in atomizing water, (2) internal tower volume for air-water contact, (3) airflow rate through tower, (4) water flow rate. Cooling tower systems are open and need makeup water to replace evaporated water or blowdowns. Closed systems use expansion tanks to compensate for volume changes due to temperature changes.

## Note ID: 73
### **SECTION 2.2 - SHELL AND TUBE HEAT EXCHANGERS**

**SECTION 2.2.1 - CROSS FLOW HEAT EXCHANGERS**
**OBJECTIVE 3: Describe the four basic flow paths for shell-and-tube heat exchangers (Cross Flow, Parallel Flow, Counter Flow, U-Tube).**
Crossflow occurs when two fluids flow perpendicular to each other. Main condensers are classic crossflow examples - exhaust steam flows downward perpendicular to tubes containing cooling water. Often used to condense or evaporate liquids. Steam at temperature T condenses to condensate at same temperature (phase change). Temperature outside condenser tubes is constant; circulating water enters at temperature (t) and exits at higher temperature. Greatest temperature difference where water enters tubes; most steam condenses at this end.

---

## Note ID: 74
### **SECTION 2.2.2 - PARALLEL FLOW HEAT EXCHANGERS**

Parallel flow occurs when fluids flow in parallel paths and same direction. NOT commonly used because material requires larger surface area than other types for given heat load. When cold fluid enters, rate of heat transfer is large because temperature difference (ΔT) is large. As heat transfers, temperatures converge and ΔT becomes smaller. Therefore, rate of heat transfer decreases as fluids travel through parallel exchanger.

---

## Note ID: 75
### **SECTION 2.2.3 - COUNTER FLOW HEAT EXCHANGERS**

**OBJECTIVE 4: Explain why a counter-flow heat exchanger will produce a higher rate of heat transfer than an equally sized parallel-flow heat exchanger.**
Counter flow heat exchangers have parallel flow paths but fluids flow in opposite directions. Temperature difference and fluid flow rates determine heat transferred. Temperature profile changes throughout exchanger. Designers use logarithmic mean temperature difference (ΔTlm) to calculate overall heat transfer rate. Heat transfer rate: Q = Uo × A × ΔT, where Q = rate of heat transfer (Btu/hr), Uo = overall heat transfer coefficient (Btu/ft² hr°F), A = surface area (ft²), ΔT = temperature difference (°F). Larger temperature difference, surface area, or heat transfer coefficient = more heat transferred. Counter flow can transfer large amount of heat for its size compared to parallel and crossflow types.

---

## Note ID: 76
### **SECTION 2.2.4 - U-TUBE HEAT EXCHANGERS**

U-tube heat exchangers extensively used in plants. May be mounted horizontally or vertically. Performance like crossflow heat exchangers. Tube side working fluid enters waterbox, directed into one end of U-tubes, flows through tubes, exits through channel head. Divider plate separates inlet and outlet sides. Shell side flow enters shell, passes over outside of tubes, exits shell. Baffles normally used to force flow to zigzag across tubes. Advantages: expansion simplified, half as many tube-to-sheet joints needed. Disadvantage: difficult to mechanically clean tube surfaces.

---

## Note ID: 77
### **SECTION 3.0 - REGENERATIVE AND NON-REGENERATIVE HEAT EXCHANGERS**

**OBJECTIVE 5: Describe regenerative and non-regenerative heat exchange processes.**
Regenerative heat exchanger transfers heat from high temperature fluid into low temperature sections of same system (example: Feedwater Heaters in secondary system). Minimizes energy loss since heat not rejected to separate system. Non-regenerative heat exchanger transfers heat between liquids in two distinct systems (examples: Turbine Lube Oil Coolers, Residual Heat Removal HX). Used to transfer heat out of particular system; heat removed is rejected and not returned. Typical application: regenerative HX in Chemical and Volume Control System reduces letdown temperature from 540°F to 250°F using charging flow returning to RCS.

---

## Note ID: 78
### **SECTION 4.0 - HEAT EXCHANGER CONSTRUCTION**

**OBJECTIVE 6: Describe major components of a heat exchanger.**
Shell and tube heat exchanger consists of: shell (casing around heat transfer region), multiple tubes forming tube bundle, tubesheets (perforated metal plates at shell ends), waterboxes or channel heads, baffles, inlet/outlet nozzles, vent and drain connections. Two separate fluids flow: tube side fluid (usually higher pressure, flows through tubes) and shell side fluid (usually lower pressure, flows outside tubes). Shell houses internals and directs shell side flow. Tubes direct and separate tube side fluid. Two common tube types: Straight tubes and U-tubes.

---

## Note ID: 79
### **SECTION 4.1 - HEAT EXCHANGER TUBES**

Tubesheets separate two fluids and provide tube support. Waterboxes enclose tubesheet areas at each end, direct flow through tube bundles, removable for cleaning. Nozzles are where fluid systems connect to shell or waterboxes. Vents remove air and non-condensable gases improving performance. Drains remove liquid before maintenance. As tubes heat up they expand - typical 30-foot feedwater heater tube expands 1.4 inches if heated from 70°F to 470°F. To prevent damage, one end of tube bundle designed to expand/contract. Floating head design allows one tubesheet to slide, absorbing expansion.

---

## Note ID: 80
### **HEAT EXCHANGER TUBE ARRANGEMENTS**

Tube types: Straight tube - arranged in bundle enclosed by shell, allows easy mechanical cleaning and replacement. U-tube - advantages include simplified expansion and half as many tube-to-sheet joints. Coil tube - one or more coils inside shell, expansion absorbed like spring, no seals needed, suitable for high pressure. Bowed tube - solidly bolted to shell at each end, bow allows thermal expansion.

---

## Note ID: 81
### **SECTION 4.2 - HEAT EXCHANGER SHELLS AND TUBE SURFACES**

Plain surface tubes are smooth (examples: condenser, feedwater heaters, lube oil coolers). Extended surfaces have fins, rings, studs, grooves or other extensions. Advantages: increase surface area for heat transfer. Gas-to-liquid heat exchangers often use extended surface tubes with gas on finned side. Shell may be oriented horizontally or vertically per operational needs. Main condenser is one of largest shell and tube HX - rectangular shaped with thousands of tubes. Most other shell and tube HX in plant are cylindrical.

---

## Note ID: 82
### **HEAT EXCHANGER PASSES AND BAFFLES**

Shell and tube HX can have any number of tube or shell passes. Pass occurs each time either fluid flows across or along heat exchanger length. Multiple passes allow shorter HX with same tube length. Shell side fluid takes shortest route from inlet to outlet without baffles. Baffles direct flow over tubes and serve as lateral support. Cause fluid to cross tubes several times while maintaining overall counter flow direction. Resulting HX has combination of cross and counter flow heat transfer.

---

## Note ID: 83
### **SECTION 5.0 - HEAT EXCHANGER OPERATION**

**SECTION 5.1 - HEAT EXCHANGER STARTUP**
**OBJECTIVE 7: Explain the following regarding Thermal Shock of a heat exchanger: Description of thermal shock, Conditions or situations that make thermal shock more likely, Operational concerns for a heat exchanger that undergoes thermal shock.**
Before operation, HX must be filled with operating fluids. Differential thermal expansion between tubes and shell minimized by gradually introducing fluids by throttling inlet valves. Warming done gradually to prevent thermal shock (temperature change inducing excessive thermal stress). Thermal shock occurs when temperature suddenly increases/decreases causing differential metal expansion/contraction, challenging integrity. After sudden temperature decrease, brittle fracture possible due to additional thermal stresses if internal pressure high.

---

## Note ID: 84
### **HEAT EXCHANGER STARTUP PROCEDURE**

To place HX in service: (1) Cold fluid circulated first using vent connections for air evacuation. Air-containing HX is less efficient since air reduces heat transfer surface area. (2) Hot fluid valved in after cold fluid to prevent thermal shock. (3) Air evacuation again accomplished using vent connections. Inlet valve throttled to limit flow and minimize pressure spiking. Once full, vent valves closed. Vent valves opened while filling to let air escape and prevent air binding. Pressure spike called water hammer occurs when fluid velocity suddenly changes. Causes pipe movement and damage. Minimized by slowly operating valves.

---

## Note ID: 85
### **SECTION 5.2 - HEAT EXCHANGER SHUTDOWN**

Procedures for shutting down heat exchangers involve controlled cooling and draining. As HX cools, metal contracts. Drains should be opened to prevent water hammer and pressure spikes. Procedures ensure safe cooling and maintenance access without damage to equipment.

---

## Note ID: 86
### **SECTION 5.3 - DYNAMICS OF HEAT EXCHANGER OPERATION**

**OBJECTIVE 8: Explain how the following operational factors affect heat exchanger performance: Flow rates that are too high or too low; Scaling or tube fouling; Heat exchanger tube failure.**
Multiple operational factors significantly impact heat exchanger performance. Flow rates that are too high create excessive pressure drops and energy loss. Flow rates too low result in inadequate heat transfer. Scaling or tube fouling deposits reduce heat transfer surface area. Deposits include mineral scale, corrosion products, biological growth. Heat exchanger tube failure can result in cross-contamination of fluids, loss of heat transfer capability, and potential operational hazards.

---

## Note ID: 87
### **HEAT TRANSFER COEFFICIENT AND SYSTEM OPTIMIZATION**

Heat transfer rate depends on U (overall heat transfer coefficient), A (surface area), and ΔT (temperature difference). Deposits reduce coefficient. Tube failure reduces effective surface area. System operators must maintain flow rates, monitor for fouling indicators, and detect tube failures through temperature, pressure, and flow monitoring. Maintaining clean surfaces and proper flow ensures optimal performance.

## Note ID: 88
### **SECTION 6.0 - CONDENSERS**

**OBJECTIVE 9: Explain the relationship between condenser vacuum and backpressure.**
Condenser vacuum and turbine backpressure are directly related. Higher vacuum in condenser means lower pressure, which creates greater pressure difference between turbine inlet and outlet. This pressure difference drives more work from steam, improving turbine efficiency. Vacuum is measured as absolute pressure. Better vacuum (lower absolute pressure) reduces backpressure, improving plant efficiency. Degraded condenser performance reduces vacuum, increasing backpressure, reducing turbine output and efficiency.

---

## Note ID: 89
### **SECTION 6.1 - CONDENSER CONSTRUCTION**

Main condenser is one of largest shell and tube heat exchangers - rectangular shaped with thousands of tubes carrying circulating water. Condenser tubes designed for efficient heat transfer with steam on shell side and cooling water on tube side. Tube bundles arranged for maximum heat transfer. Hotwell collects condensed steam. Condenser designed as crossflow with steam flowing downward perpendicular to tube bundles containing circulating water.

---

## Note ID: 90
### **CONDENSER VACUUM SYSTEM**

Condenser vacuum maintained by air removal systems. Non-condensable gases (primarily air) cannot condense and accumulate in condenser. Buildup of non-condensable gases increases pressure and reduces vacuum. Air removal systems (jet air ejectors or vacuum pumps) continuously remove non-condensables, maintaining vacuum. Effective air removal is critical for sustained condenser performance and efficient operation.

---

## Note ID: 91
### **SECTION 6.2 - CONDENSER OPERATION**

**OBJECTIVE 10: Explain the principle of operation of condensers.**
Condenser operation depends on steam contacting cold condenser tubes where condensation occurs. As exhaust steam contacts cold condenser tubes it condenses, forming condensate. Condensate falls and collects in hotwell, then pumped through secondary plant components requiring cooling and to feedwater heater trains. Circulating water through tubes keeps them cold and provides condensation surface. Latent heat of condensation transferred through tube walls, warming circulating water. Large quantities of cooling water needed - approximately 65 to 100 pounds or 8 to 12 gallons of cooling water needed per pound of steam condensed.

---

## Note ID: 92
### **CONDENSER PERFORMANCE MONITORING**

Condenser efficiency monitored by measuring vacuum, outlet water temperature, and flow rates. Degradation indicated by: (1) Reduced vacuum despite stable load, (2) Increased outlet water temperature, (3) Fouling of tubes, (4) Air in-leakage. Regular monitoring detects performance degradation early, allowing corrective action before significant efficiency loss. Maintenance includes tube cleaning and air system maintenance.
