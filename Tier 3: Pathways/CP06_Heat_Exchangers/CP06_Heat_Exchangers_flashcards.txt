FRONT: What is the main difference between direct and indirect contact heat exchangers?
BACK: Direct: fluids touch each other. Indirect: fluids separated by solid surface (tube/plate).

[Note ID: 187]
FRONT: What are common examples of direct contact heat exchangers?
BACK: Cooling towers, lakes, rivers. Fluids in direct contact.

[Note ID: 188]
FRONT: What are common examples of indirect contact heat exchangers?
BACK: Steam generators, main condensers. Fluids separated by surfaces.

[Note ID: 189]
FRONT: How does a forced draft cooling tower work?
BACK: Fans blow air into tower to exchange heat between water and air.

[Note ID: 190]
FRONT: How does a natural draft cooling tower work?
BACK: Convective chimney effect (hot air rises) pulls air through. No fans needed.

[Note ID: 191]
FRONT: What two flow configurations are used in cooling towers?
BACK: Cross-flow (air enters side, exits opposite) and Counter-flow (air enters bottom, exits top).

[Note ID: 192]
FRONT: What is the purpose of cooling tower fill material?
BACK: Increases air-water contact surface area. Better heat transfer. Usually PVC/plastic.

[Note ID: 193]
FRONT: What are the four basic flow paths in shell-and-tube heat exchangers?
BACK: Cross flow, Parallel flow, Counter flow, U-tube.

[Note ID: 194]
FRONT: What is crossflow heat transfer?
BACK: Fluids flow perpendicular to each other. Example: main condensers.

[Note ID: 195]
FRONT: What is parallel flow heat transfer?
BACK: Fluids flow in same direction. Requires large surface area. Rarely used.

[Note ID: 196]
FRONT: What is counter flow heat transfer?
BACK: Fluids flow in opposite directions. Most efficient. Maintains large temp difference.

[Note ID: 197]
FRONT: Why is counter-flow more efficient than parallel-flow?
BACK: Maintains larger temp difference throughout, allows more heat transfer in smaller space.

[Note ID: 198]
FRONT: What is the formula for heat transfer rate?
BACK: Q = Uo × A × ΔT. (coefficient × area × temp difference).

[Note ID: 199]
FRONT: What are three factors that increase heat transfer rate?
BACK: Larger Uo (coefficient), larger A (area), larger ΔT (temperature difference).

[Note ID: 200]
FRONT: What is a regenerative heat exchanger?
BACK: Transfers heat from hot to cold sections of SAME system. Example: Feedwater Heaters. Minimizes energy loss.

[Note ID: 201]
FRONT: What is a non-regenerative heat exchanger?
BACK: Transfers heat between TWO SEPARATE systems. Heat rejected/discarded. Example: Lube Oil Coolers.

[Note ID: 202]
FRONT: What are the major components of shell-and-tube heat exchangers?
BACK: Shell, tube bundle, tubesheets, waterboxes, baffles, nozzles, vents, drains.

[Note ID: 203]
FRONT: What is the shell in a heat exchanger?
BACK: Casing surrounding heat transfer region. Houses shell-side fluid.

[Note ID: 204]
FRONT: What are tubesheets?
BACK: Perforated metal plates at shell ends. Separate fluids and support tubes.

[Note ID: 205]
FRONT: What are baffles?
BACK: Internal dividers directing shell-side flow to cross tubes multiple times.

[Note ID: 206]
FRONT: What are two common tube types?
BACK: Straight tubes (easy clean/replace) and U-tubes (simplified expansion, fewer joints).

[Note ID: 207]
FRONT: What is thermal shock in a heat exchanger?
BACK: Sudden temp change causing excessive thermal stress. Risk of cracking/brittle fracture.

[Note ID: 208]
FRONT: When is thermal shock most likely?
BACK: Startup: hot/cold fluids suddenly introduced. Sudden temp changes during operation.

[Note ID: 209]
FRONT: How do you prevent thermal shock during startup?
BACK: Gradually introduce fluids by throttling inlet valves. Minimize sudden temp changes.

[Note ID: 210]
FRONT: What is the startup procedure for a heat exchanger?
BACK: (1) Circulate cold fluid with vents open. (2) Slowly valve in hot fluid. (3) Throttle inlet to prevent pressure spikes.

[Note ID: 211]
FRONT: What is water hammer in heat exchangers?
BACK: Pressure spike when fluid velocity suddenly changes. Causes pipe movement/damage.

[Note ID: 212]
FRONT: How do you minimize water hammer?
BACK: Operate valves slowly. Avoid sudden velocity changes.

[Note ID: 213]
FRONT: How do flow rates affect heat exchanger performance?
BACK: Too high: excessive pressure drops, energy loss. Too low: inadequate heat transfer. Must optimize.

[Note ID: 214]
FRONT: What is scaling in a heat exchanger?
BACK: Deposit buildup (mineral scale, corrosion). Reduces heat transfer surface/efficiency.

[Note ID: 215]
FRONT: What causes tube fouling?
BACK: Mineral deposits, corrosion products, biological growth. Reduces coefficient/area.

[Note ID: 216]
FRONT: What happens if a heat exchanger tube fails?
BACK: Cross-contamination of fluids. Loss of heat transfer. Operational hazards.

[Note ID: 217]
FRONT: How do you maintain heat exchanger performance?
BACK: Keep surfaces clean. Maintain proper flow. Monitor fouling. Detect failures early.

[Note ID: 218]
FRONT: What is the relationship between condenser vacuum and turbine backpressure?
BACK: Higher vacuum (lower pressure) = lower backpressure = greater pressure difference = more turbine work/efficiency.

[Note ID: 219]
FRONT: Why is condenser vacuum important?
BACK: Better vacuum improves turbine efficiency. Increases pressure difference. More steam work.

[Note ID: 220]
FRONT: What happens when condenser vacuum degrades?
BACK: Backpressure increases. Turbine output decreases. Plant efficiency reduces.

[Note ID: 221]
FRONT: How is vacuum measured in condensers?
BACK: As absolute pressure. Lower absolute pressure = better vacuum.

[Note ID: 222]
FRONT: How does a main condenser operate?
BACK: Exhaust steam contacts cold tubes → condenses to condensate. Cooling water absorbs latent heat.

[Note ID: 223]
FRONT: What is the flow pattern in a main condenser?
BACK: Crossflow: steam flows down perpendicular to cooling water tubes.

[Note ID: 224]
FRONT: What is the hotwell?
BACK: Collects condensed steam at condenser bottom. Pumped to feedwater system.

[Note ID: 225]
FRONT: How much cooling water is needed per pound of steam?
BACK: 65-100 lbm (8-12 gallons) cooling water per lbm steam condensed.

[Note ID: 226]
FRONT: What are non-condensable gases?
BACK: Air and other gases that cannot condense. Accumulate and increase pressure. Reduce vacuum.

[Note ID: 227]
FRONT: How do air removal systems work?
BACK: Jet air ejectors or vacuum pumps remove non-condensable gases continuously.

[Note ID: 228]
FRONT: How do you monitor condenser efficiency?
BACK: Measure vacuum, outlet water temp, flow rates. Degradation: reduced vacuum, higher outlet temp.

[Note ID: 229]
FRONT: What is biofouling?
BACK: Aquatic organism growth. Degrades cooling tower/condenser. Controlled by chemical treatment.

[Note ID: 230]
FRONT: What are three key principles of heat exchanger operation?
BACK: (1) Heat transfers hot → cold. (2) Temp difference drives transfer. (3) Minimize losses through design/maintenance.

[Note ID: 231]
FRONT: What makes an effective heat exchanger?
BACK: Proper flow patterns, adequate surface area, clean surfaces, correct fluid properties, good thermal design.

[Note ID: 232]
FRONT: What is an operator's role in heat exchanger performance?
BACK: Monitor conditions, maintain flow, prevent fouling, detect failures, proper startup/shutdown.
