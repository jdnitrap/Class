HEAT EXCHANGERS - INTRODUCTION: A heat exchanger is a device that is designed to transfer heat from one fluid to another. Two key concepts: (1) Heat can only be transferred when there is a temperature difference between substances; (2) Heat always transfers from higher temperature substance to lower temperature substance. Heat is transferred primarily by conduction (heat transfer between adjacent molecules through material) and convection (heat transfer by bulk motion and mixing of fluid portions).

[Note ID: 2]
DIRECT CONTACT VS INDIRECT CONTACT HEAT EXCHANGERS: Direct contact heat exchangers allow two fluids to come in contact with each other (examples: cooling towers, lakes, rivers). Indirect contact (surface) heat exchangers separate fluids by a solid surface like tubes or metal plates (examples: steam generators, main condensers, feedwater heaters). Direct contact is less common; indirect contact is more commonly used in power plants.

[Note ID: 3]
COOLING TOWERS: Cooling towers exchange energy from cooling water into the air flowing through the tower using evaporative cooling. The cooled circulating water serves as heat sink for main condensers. Two main types: open-circuit and closed-circuit. Two basic flow configurations: Cross-flow (air enters side, flows across, exits opposite side) and Counter flow (air enters bottom, flows upward; water enters top, flows downward). Towers may be forced-draft (fans blow air in), induced-draft (fans draw air through), or natural-draft (uses convective chimney effects).

[Note ID: 4]
COOLING TOWER FILL MATERIAL AND DESIGN: Tower fill material was traditionally redwood but now mostly PVC or similar plastic. Critical factors for efficiency: (1) effectiveness of spray or splash in atomizing water, (2) internal tower volume for air-water contact, (3) airflow rate through tower, (4) water flow rate. Cooling tower systems are open and need makeup water to replace evaporated water or blowdowns. Closed systems use expansion tanks to compensate for volume changes due to temperature changes.

[Note ID: 5]
CROSSFLOW HEAT EXCHANGERS: Crossflow occurs when two fluids flow perpendicular to each other. Main condensers are classic crossflow examples - exhaust steam flows downward perpendicular to tubes containing cooling water. Often used to condense or evaporate liquids. Steam at temperature T condenses to condensate at same temperature (phase change, not temperature change). Temperature on outside condenser tubes is constant; circulating water enters at temperature (t) and exits at higher temperature. Greatest temperature difference where water enters tubes; most steam condenses at this end. As water temperature increases along tube, less heat is transferred.

[Note ID: 6]
PARALLEL FLOW HEAT EXCHANGERS: Parallel flow occurs when fluids flow in parallel paths and same direction. NOT commonly used because material requires larger surface area than other types for given heat load. When cold fluid enters, rate of heat transfer is large because temperature difference (ΔT) is large. As heat transfers, temperatures converge and ΔT becomes smaller. Therefore, rate of heat transfer decreases as fluids travel through parallel exchanger.

[Note ID: 7]
COUNTER FLOW HEAT EXCHANGERS: Counter flow heat exchangers have parallel flow paths but fluids flow in opposite directions. Temperature difference and fluid flow rates determine heat transferred. Temperature profile changes throughout exchanger. Designers use logarithmic mean temperature difference (ΔTlm) to calculate overall heat transfer rate. For ease of calculation, ΔTlm can be substituted with ΔT (difference between shell side average temperature and tube side average temperature). Heat transfer rate: Q = Uo × A × ΔT, where Q = rate of heat transfer (Btu/hr), Uo = overall heat transfer coefficient (Btu/ft² hr°F), A = surface area (ft²), ΔT = temperature difference (°F). Larger temperature difference, surface area, or heat transfer coefficient = more heat transferred. Counter flow can transfer large amount of heat for its size compared to parallel and crossflow types. Most shell and tube heat exchangers are counter flow type.

[Note ID: 8]
U-TUBE HEAT EXCHANGERS: Extensively used in plants. May be mounted horizontally or vertically. Performance like crossflow heat exchangers. Tube side working fluid enters waterbox (channel head), directed into one end of U-tubes, flows through tubes, exits through channel head. Divider plate separates inlet and outlet sides. Shell side flow enters shell, passes over outside of tubes, exits shell. Baffles normally used to force flow to zigzag across tubes. Advantage: expansion problem simplified and half as many tube-to-tube sheet joints needed (only one tubesheet, rigidly attached to shell). Tubes free to move within shell when heated/cooled. Disadvantage: difficult to mechanically clean tube surfaces.

[Note ID: 9]
REGENERATIVE VS NON-REGENERATIVE HEAT EXCHANGERS: Regenerative heat exchanger transfers heat from high temperature fluid into low temperature sections of same system (example: Feedwater Heaters in secondary system). Minimizes energy loss since heat not rejected to separate system. Non-regenerative heat exchanger transfers heat between liquids in two distinct systems (examples: Turbine Lube Oil Coolers, Residual Heat Removal HX). Used to transfer heat out of particular system; heat removed is rejected and not returned. Typical application: regenerative HX in Chemical and Volume Control System reduces letdown temperature from 540°F to 250°F using charging flow returning to RCS.

[Note ID: 10]
HEAT EXCHANGER COMPONENTS: Shell and tube heat exchanger consists of: shell (casing around heat transfer region), multiple tubes forming tube bundle, tubesheets (perforated metal plates at shell ends), waterboxes or channel heads, baffles, inlet/outlet nozzles, vent and drain connections. Two separate fluids flow: tube side fluid (usually higher pressure, flows through tubes) and shell side fluid (usually lower pressure, flows outside tubes). Shell houses internals and directs shell side flow. Tubes direct and separate tube side fluid. Two common tube types: Straight tubes and U-tubes.

[Note ID: 11]
HEAT EXCHANGER TUBES AND EXPANSION: Tubesheets separate two fluids and provide tube support. Waterboxes enclose tubesheet areas at each end, direct flow through tube bundles, removable for cleaning. Nozzles are where fluid systems connect to shell or waterboxes. Vents remove air and non-condensable gases improving performance. Drains remove liquid before maintenance. As tubes heat up they expand - typical 30-foot feedwater heater tube expands 1.4 inches if heated from 70°F to 470°F. To prevent damage, one end of tube bundle designed to expand/contract. Floating head design allows one tubesheet to slide, absorbing expansion.

[Note ID: 12]
HEAT EXCHANGER TUBE ARRANGEMENTS: Straight tube - arranged in bundle enclosed by shell, allows easy mechanical cleaning and easy replacement, normally has one floating head. U-tube - two advantages: (1) expansion simplified, (2) half as many tube-to-sheet joints needed (one tubesheet, rigidly attached). Tubes free to move within shell when heated/cooled. Disadvantage: difficult mechanical cleaning. Coil tube - one or more coils inside shell, expansion absorbed like spring, no seals/gaskets needed between floating head and shell, suitable for high pressure, usually small, often used for sample coolers. Bowed tube - solidly bolted to shell at each end, bow allows thermal expansion, advantage: scale buildup cracks off in flexing sections as tubes bend during cooling.

[Note ID: 13]
HEAT EXCHANGER TUBE SURFACES: Plain surface tubes are smooth (examples: condenser, feedwater heaters, lube oil coolers). Extended surfaces have fins, rings, studs, grooves or other extensions (Figure 10). Advantages: increase surface area for heat transfer. Gas-to-liquid heat exchangers often use extended surface tubes with gas on finned side. Gases need larger surface area because they have lower heat transfer coefficients. Examples of extended surface HX: hydrogen coolers, economizers, room heaters/coolers.

[Note ID: 14]
HEAT EXCHANGER SHELLS AND PASSES: Main condenser is one of largest shell and tube HX - rectangular shaped with thousands of tubes carrying circulating water. Most other shell and tube HX in plant are cylindrical. Shell may be oriented horizontally or vertically per operational needs. Shell and tube HX can have any number of tube or shell passes. Pass occurs each time either fluid flows across or along heat exchanger length. Multiple passes allow shorter HX with same tube length. Single-pass tube/single-pass shell vs 2-pass tube/single-pass shell vs 2-pass tube/2-pass shell vs 4-pass tube/2-pass shell configurations available.

[Note ID: 15]
HEAT EXCHANGER BAFFLES: Shell side fluid takes shortest route (path of least resistance) from inlet to outlet without baffles. This doesn't fully utilize tube surfaces. Baffles direct flow over tubes and serve as lateral support. Cause fluid to cross tubes several times while maintaining overall counter flow direction. Resulting HX has combination of cross and counter flow heat transfer. Two possible baffle arrangements exist.

[Note ID: 16]
HEAT EXCHANGER STARTUP - THERMAL SHOCK AND WATER HAMMER: Before operation, HX must be filled with operating fluids. Differential thermal expansion between tubes and shell minimized by gradually introducing fluids by throttling inlet valves. Warming done gradually to prevent thermal shock (temperature change inducing excessive thermal stress). Thermal shock occurs when temperature suddenly increases/decreases causing differential metal expansion/contraction, challenging integrity. After sudden temperature decrease, brittle fracture possible due to additional thermal stresses if internal pressure high. Vent valves opened while filling to let air escape and prevent air binding. Pressure spike called water hammer occurs when fluid velocity suddenly changes. Causes pipe movement and damage to pipes, hangers, valves. Caused by starting/stopping pumps or quickly opening/closing valves. Minimized by slowly operating valves and keeping systems filled and vented.

[Note ID: 17]
HEAT EXCHANGER STARTUP PROCEDURE: To place HX in service: (1) Cold fluid circulated first using vent connections for air evacuation. Air-containing HX is less efficient (air reduces heat transfer surface area). (2) Hot fluid valved in after cold fluid to prevent thermal shock. (3) Air evacuation again accomplished using vent connections. Inlet valve throttled to limit flow and minimize pressure spiking. Once full, vent valves closed.

[Note ID: 18]
HEAT EXCHANGER SHUTDOWN: Hot fluid flow gradually reduced to zero. Then cold fluid stopped. For HX with cold fluid significantly cooler than hot fluid or cooler than ambient, flow stopped quickly (minimizes thermal contraction during cooling). HX then drained or placed in wet lay-up (filled to vents, vents open). Draining accomplished by opening vent valves then drain valves. Wet lay-up used if HX returned to service after short time and doesn't require maintenance.

[Note ID: 19]
HEAT EXCHANGER DYNAMICS AND PERFORMANCE: Dynamic operation depends on many factors. Equations for temperature/enthalpy change assume overall heat transfer coefficient constant throughout. Coefficient depends on convective heat transfer both sides and conductive transfer through tube walls. Conductive transfer determined by tube thermal conductivity and ΔT across walls (temperature-dependent). ΔT changes along HX length. Convective heat transfer determined by convection coefficients both sides. Coefficient higher for higher mass flow rates, higher specific heat capacity fluids, higher thermal conductivity fluids. For given system, easily controlled parameter is mass flow rate. For steady state with no phase change: m₁ × cp₁ × ΔT₁ = m₂ × cp₂ × ΔT₂. Assuming water both sides with constant identical cp, rearranged: Tout2 = Tin2 - (m₁ × cp₁ × ΔT₁)/(m₂ × cp₂).

[Note ID: 20]
HEAT EXCHANGER FLOW CONTROL: Hot fluid outlet temperature (Tout2) decreased by increasing cold fluid flow (m₁). For given mass flow rates, hot fluid outlet temperature changes same manner as hot fluid inlet temperature (if Tin2 increases, Tout2 increases). Effect on hot outlet temperature when cold inlet changes: as cold inlet temperature decreases, cold outlet increases (heat transfers from hot to cold), hot outlet decreases. As Tin1 decreases, Tout2 decreases. As cold mass flow rate increases: cold outlet temperature decreases, average ΔT increases, heat transfer rate increases, hot outlet temperature decreases. Increasing hot mass flow rate increases average ΔT, heat transfer rate increases, cold outlet temperature increases. Decreasing either flow produces opposite effect. Summary: decrease hot outlet by increasing cold flow rate or decreasing hot flow rate, or decrease hot inlet temperature or cold inlet temperature.

[Note ID: 21]
HEAT EXCHANGER FLOW RATE EFFECTS - LAMINAR VS TURBULENT: Flow rate important in determining HX performance. At low flows in laminar region, heat transfer by conduction from metal surface and through fluid, leading to thermal stratification. Stratification reduces performance (part of fluid not actively participating). Low velocity allows more time for scale and sediment deposits. When flow increases enough for turbulent region, heat transfer increases greatly and differential pressure increases. Heat transfer increases because convective heat transfer now occurs. Differential pressure increases due to turbulence. Turbulence and high flow help remove scale deposits and prevent materials from settling.

[Note ID: 22]
HEAT EXCHANGER FOULING AND DEPOSITS: Accumulation of solid material in HX known as fouling. Restricts flow and increases pressure drop. Heat transfer decreased as fouling builds on surfaces. Scale - deposits due to inorganic salts coming out of solution on heat transfer surfaces. Biofouling - due to growth of marine animals or plants (zebra mussels particular concern in some plants). Increasing mass flow rate decreases scale formation. Unfortunately, higher velocity leads to higher tube erosion and vibration. Tube erosion - wearing away of tube surfaces from flow. Vibration may cause tubes to contact other tubes or baffles, leading to denting and fretting wear (tube material chipped off at contact points). Vibration leads to fatigue failure at fixed position points (tubesheet joints, baffles, supports) from repeated cyclic stress application.

[Note ID: 23]
HEAT EXCHANGER TUBE FAILURE CONSEQUENCES: Tube failure allows higher-pressure fluid to mix with lower-pressure fluid. Results in loss of (fluid) inventory from high-pressure system. If high-pressure fluid contains radioactive materials, release to environment may occur. If high-pressure fluid in tubes, shell may rupture if design pressure exceeded. Tube failure in condenser causes decreased condenser vacuum (increased absolute pressure) from water in-leakage. Intrusion of impurities from circulating water (chlorides) increases condensate conductivity and increases corrosion in condensate system. Tube failure results in mixing of fluids, loss of high-pressure fluid inventory (tank level drops), temperature increase in low-pressure fluid, plus possible radioactive or chemical impurity releases.

[Note ID: 24]
MAIN CONDENSER FUNCTION AND DESIGN: Main condenser is large, vacuum-tight shell and tube heat exchanger. Low-pressure steam exhausted over tubes; circulating water flows through tubes. Shell side called steam side; tubes and water boxes make up water side. Two main functions: (1) provide high vacuum environment for turbine exhaust steam, (2) condense turbine exhaust steam for reuse in power cycle. For good plant efficiency, steam side operates in vacuum. Low turbine exhaust pressure allows steam through turbine to do more work. Large volume reduction when steam condenses produces vacuum. Vacuum expressed in inches of mercury absolute, equal to atmospheric pressure (29.92 in Hg abs or 14.7 psia) minus condenser vacuum, called condenser backpressure.

[Note ID: 25]
CONDENSER OPERATION AND COOLING REQUIREMENTS: As exhaust steam contacts cold condenser tubes it condenses, forming condensate. Condensate falls and collects in hotwell, then pumped through secondary plant components requiring cooling, and to feedwater heater trains. Circulating water through tubes keeps them cold and provides condensation surface. Latent heat of condensation transferred through tube walls, warming circulating water. Large quantities of cooling water needed - approximately 65 to 100 pounds or 8 to 12 gallons of cooling water needed per pound of steam condensed.
