OBJECTIVE 1: What is the main difference between direct contact and indirect contact heat exchangers?
Direct contact: fluids touch each other. Indirect contact: fluids separated by solid surface (tube/plate).

[Note ID: 187]
What are common examples of direct contact heat exchangers?
Cooling towers, lakes, rivers - fluids in direct contact.

[Note ID: 188]
What are common examples of indirect contact heat exchangers?
Steam generators, main condensers - fluids separated by surfaces.

[Note ID: 189]
OBJECTIVE 2: How does a forced draft cooling tower work?
Fans blow air into the tower to exchange heat between cooling water and air.

[Note ID: 190]
How does a natural draft cooling tower work?
Uses convective chimney effects (hot air rises) to pull air through tower - no fans needed.

[Note ID: 191]
What two flow configurations are used in cooling towers?
Cross-flow (air enters side, exits opposite) and Counter-flow (air enters bottom, exits top).

[Note ID: 192]
What is the purpose of cooling tower fill material?
Increases air-water contact surface area for better heat transfer. Usually PVC or plastic.

[Note ID: 193]
OBJECTIVE 3: What are the four basic flow paths in shell-and-tube heat exchangers?
Cross flow, Parallel flow, Counter flow, U-tube.

[Note ID: 194]
What is crossflow heat transfer?
Two fluids flow perpendicular to each other. Example: main condensers.

[Note ID: 195]
What is parallel flow heat transfer?
Two fluids flow in same direction. Requires large surface area - not commonly used.

[Note ID: 196]
What is counter flow heat transfer?
Two fluids flow in opposite directions. Most efficient - maintains large temperature difference.

[Note ID: 197]
OBJECTIVE 4: Why is counter-flow more efficient than parallel-flow?
Counter-flow maintains larger temperature difference throughout exchanger, allowing more heat transfer in smaller space.

[Note ID: 198]
What formula calculates heat transfer rate?
Q = Uo × A × ΔT (heat transfer = coefficient × surface area × temperature difference)

[Note ID: 199]
What three factors increase heat transfer rate?
Larger overall heat transfer coefficient (Uo), larger surface area (A), larger temperature difference (ΔT).

[Note ID: 200]
OBJECTIVE 5: What is a regenerative heat exchanger?
Transfers heat from hot fluid into cold sections of SAME system. Example: Feedwater Heaters. Minimizes energy loss.

[Note ID: 201]
What is a non-regenerative heat exchanger?
Transfers heat between TWO SEPARATE systems. Heat is rejected and discarded. Example: Turbine Lube Oil Coolers.

[Note ID: 202]
OBJECTIVE 6: What are the major components of a shell and tube heat exchanger?
Shell, tube bundle, tubesheets, waterboxes, baffles, nozzles, vents, drains.

[Note ID: 203]
What is the shell in a heat exchanger?
The casing that surrounds the heat transfer region. Houses shell-side fluid.

[Note ID: 204]
What are tubesheets in a heat exchanger?
Perforated metal plates at shell ends that separate the two fluids and support tubes.

[Note ID: 205]
What are baffles in a heat exchanger?
Internal dividers that direct shell-side flow to cross tubes multiple times for better heat transfer.

[Note ID: 206]
What are the two common tube types?
Straight tubes (easy to clean/replace) and U-tubes (simplified expansion, fewer joints).

[Note ID: 207]
OBJECTIVE 7: What is thermal shock in a heat exchanger?
Sudden temperature change causing excessive thermal stress - risk of metal cracking or brittle fracture.

[Note ID: 208]
When is thermal shock most likely to occur?
During startup when hot and cold fluids suddenly introduced, or sudden temperature changes during operation.

[Note ID: 209]
How do you prevent thermal shock during heat exchanger startup?
Gradually introduce fluids by throttling inlet valves - minimize sudden temperature changes.

[Note ID: 210]
What is the startup procedure for a heat exchanger?
(1) Circulate cold fluid first with vents open. (2) Valve in hot fluid slowly. (3) Throttle inlet to prevent pressure spikes.

[Note ID: 211]
What is water hammer in heat exchangers?
Pressure spike when fluid velocity suddenly changes - causes pipe movement and damage.

[Note ID: 212]
How do you minimize water hammer?
Operate valves slowly - avoid sudden changes in fluid velocity.

[Note ID: 213]
OBJECTIVE 8: How do flow rates affect heat exchanger performance?
Too high = excessive pressure drops and energy loss. Too low = inadequate heat transfer. Must optimize.

[Note ID: 214]
What is scaling in a heat exchanger?
Deposit buildup (mineral scale, corrosion products) that reduces heat transfer surface area and efficiency.

[Note ID: 215]
What causes tube fouling in heat exchangers?
Deposits from minerals, corrosion, biological growth that reduce heat transfer coefficient and surface area.

[Note ID: 216]
What happens if a heat exchanger tube fails?
Cross-contamination of fluids, loss of heat transfer capability, and potential operational hazards.

[Note ID: 217]
How do you maintain heat exchanger performance?
Keep surfaces clean, maintain proper flow rates, monitor for fouling, detect tube failures early.

[Note ID: 218]
OBJECTIVE 9: What is the relationship between condenser vacuum and turbine backpressure?
Higher vacuum (lower pressure) = lower backpressure = greater pressure difference across turbine = more work and efficiency.

[Note ID: 219]
Why is condenser vacuum important?
Better vacuum improves turbine efficiency by increasing pressure difference, allowing more steam work conversion.

[Note ID: 220]
What happens when condenser vacuum degrades?
Backpressure increases, turbine output decreases, plant efficiency reduces.

[Note ID: 221]
How is vacuum measured in condensers?
As absolute pressure - lower absolute pressure = better vacuum.

[Note ID: 222]
OBJECTIVE 10: How does a main condenser operate?
Exhaust steam from turbine contacts cold condenser tubes - steam condenses to condensate - circulating water absorbs latent heat.

[Note ID: 223]
What is the flow pattern in a main condenser?
Crossflow: exhaust steam flows downward perpendicular to tubes containing circulating cooling water.

[Note ID: 224]
What is the hotwell in a condenser?
Collects condensed steam at bottom of condenser before being pumped to feedwater system.

[Note ID: 225]
How much cooling water is needed per pound of steam?
Approximately 65 to 100 pounds (8 to 12 gallons) of cooling water per pound of steam condensed.

[Note ID: 226]
What are non-condensable gases in a condenser?
Air and other gases that cannot condense - accumulate and increase pressure, reducing vacuum.

[Note ID: 227]
How do air removal systems maintain condenser vacuum?
Jet air ejectors or vacuum pumps continuously remove non-condensable gases.

[Note ID: 228]
How do you monitor condenser efficiency?
Measure vacuum, outlet water temperature, flow rates. Degradation = reduced vacuum, increased outlet temperature.

[Note ID: 229]
What is biofouling in cooling systems?
Aquatic organism growth that degrades cooling tower and condenser performance - control through chemical treatment.

[Note ID: 230]
SUMMARY: What are the three key principles of heat exchanger operation?
(1) Heat transfers from hot to cold, (2) Temperature difference drives transfer, (3) Minimize losses through proper design/maintenance.

[Note ID: 231]
SUMMARY: What makes an effective heat exchanger?
Proper flow patterns, adequate surface area, clean surfaces, correct fluid properties, effective thermal design.

[Note ID: 232]
SUMMARY: What is the operator's role in heat exchanger performance?
Monitor conditions, maintain flow rates, prevent fouling, detect failures, perform proper startup/shutdown procedures.
